POV: giving "simple" instructions to your friend with ADHD 😅

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"Hey, can you do me a quick favor? Just grab my charger from upstairs."

Simple, right?

Wrong.

[B-ROLL]

Because what YOU just said and what an ADHD brain just HEARD are two completely different sentences.

You said: "Grab the charger."

The ADHD brain heard: "Walk upstairs. Forget why you're going upstairs. Find a book you haven't seen in three years. Read the entire back cover. Come back down. Empty-handed. And confused about why you're standing here."

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever watched someone with ADHD turn a 10-second task into a 40-minute adventure... this one's for you.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you.

When you give a person with ADHD a "simple" instruction, you think you're handing them a to-do list.

[B-ROLL]

But their brain doesn't process it like a list.

It processes it like a *maze*. With trap doors. And random detours. And a boss battle you didn't know existed.

[B-ROLL]

And the frustrating part? They *want* to do the task. They're not being lazy. They're not ignoring you. Their brain just... loses the signal halfway through.

So today I'm going to show you exactly what happens inside an ADHD brain when you say "just do this real quick" — step by step.

By the end, you'll understand why "simple" isn't simple for us. And more importantly, how to give instructions that actually *stick*.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: The instruction disappears the second you finish saying it

Here's the first problem.

For most people, hearing an instruction is like writing it in pen. It stays.

For an ADHD brain, it's like writing it in the air. It's there for a second. Then... gone.

[B-ROLL]

This is working memory. It's the brain's sticky note. And in ADHD, that sticky note is tiny, and the glue barely works.

So you say, "Can you grab my charger, and while you're up there, close the window?"

By the time we hit the top of the stairs, we've got half of one task and none of the other.

[B-ROLL]

It's not that we weren't listening. We heard every word. We just can't *hold* every word.

So what happens? We stand there. Trying to reboot. Like a phone with 2% battery.

[B-ROLL]

And that leads us straight into problem number two.

Section 2: One task turns into a chain reaction of distractions

Okay. Let's say we DO remember the task. We're heading upstairs. Charger. Got it. Locked in.

But the path to the charger is a *minefield*.

[B-ROLL]

We walk past the mirror. "Oh, my hair looks weird today."

We see a pile of clothes. "I should really fold these."

We spot a book. "Wait, I never finished this."

[B-ROLL]

Every single object in that room is basically screaming for our attention at the same volume. The charger. The laundry. The random receipt on the desk. They're all equally loud.

Neurotypical brains have a filter that dims the background stuff. ADHD brains? The filter's broken. Everything is foreground.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like walking through a store where every product has a flashing "SALE" sign. How are you supposed to remember you came in for milk?

So twenty minutes later, we're sitting on the floor, reorganizing a drawer we didn't know we cared about.

[B-ROLL]

And the charger? Still plugged in. Untouched. Living its best life.

Section 3: "Quick" is a trap word

Here's a phrase that ruins us.

"Just do it real quick."

[B-ROLL]

Because "quick" doesn't feel quick to an ADHD brain. Starting a task — *any* task — takes activation energy. And that energy is expensive for us.

It's like asking a car to start in the freezing cold. It's not that the car's broken. It just needs a few tries to turn over.

[B-ROLL]

So when you say "quick," what we hear is: "Drop everything, switch gears instantly, and perform on demand."

And switching gears is the hardest thing for us to do.

[B-ROLL]

That's why we sometimes freeze. Or snap. Or say "one second" forty-seven times. It's not attitude. It's the brain slamming on the brakes and grinding into a new gear.

[B-ROLL]

So the fix isn't to push harder. It's to give us a runway.

Which brings me to the good part.

Section 4: How to actually get through to an ADHD brain

Alright. Enough of the problems. Here's what actually works. Save this part.

Number one: One instruction at a time.

Don't stack tasks. "Grab the charger AND close the window AND bring down the cups" is three separate missions.

[B-ROLL]

Give us one. Wait. Then give us the next.

[B-ROLL]

Number two: Make it visible.

Say it AND write it. A text. A sticky note. A voice memo. Anything that lives outside our head, because inside our head it evaporates.

[B-ROLL]

Out of sight, out of mind is not a saying for us. It's a lifestyle.

Number three: Give us a heads-up before switching us.

Instead of "do this right now," try "when you finish that, can you grab the charger?"

[B-ROLL]

That tiny buffer lets the brain shift gears without stalling.

Number four — and this is the big one: don't take it personally.

[B-ROLL]

When we forget, or wander off, or come back with the wrong thing — it's not disrespect. It's not "you don't matter." It's a brain that's genuinely doing its best with a filing system that's on fire.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: The part people miss about the ADHD brain

Here's what I really want you to walk away with.

That same brain that "can't grab a charger" without getting distracted?

[B-ROLL]

It's also the brain that hyperfocuses for six hours on something it loves. That connects two ideas nobody else would connect. That comes up with the wild, creative solution in the meeting.

[B-ROLL]

The distraction and the brilliance? Same wiring. You don't get one without the other.

[B-ROLL]

So the goal was never to fix us into a boring, orderly, charger-fetching robot.

The goal is to understand how we run — and work *with* the wiring instead of against it.

[B-ROLL]

Because when someone finally *gets* how our brain works?

That's when everything changes.
