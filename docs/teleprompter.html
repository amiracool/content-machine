POV: Dopamine Trying to Negotiate With Your ADHD Brain About Starting the One Task You've Avoided for Six Days 🤝

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

Dopamine: "Okay. It's day six. We need to talk about the email."

ADHD Brain: "What email?"

Dopamine: "You know exactly what email."

ADHD Brain: "...I don't see any email."

[B-ROLL]

Dopamine: "It takes four minutes. FOUR minutes."

ADHD Brain: "Then it can wait four more days."

If you've ever avoided a two-minute task so hard it became a personality trait, stay right here. Because this negotiation? This is happening in your head every single day. And today I'm gonna show you why — and how to actually win it.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

Here's the situation. There's a task. It's not even hard. Reply to a text. Book the dentist. Submit the form.

And logically? You know it's small. You know you'll feel amazing after. You've *told* yourself that. Out loud. Multiple times.

But every time you go to start it, something in your brain slams the brakes. And instead you'll do literally anything else. Reorganize your spice rack. Learn about the Roman Empire. Suddenly become passionate about houseplants.

[B-ROLL]

This isn't laziness. It's not a character flaw. This is a dopamine problem — a real, neurological one. And once you understand *how* the negotiation actually works, you can stop losing it.

So here's what you're getting in the next few minutes: why your brain treats a four-minute task like a burning building, and three ways to hack the negotiation so the task actually gets done. Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Your Brain Doesn't See "Small Task." It Sees "No Reward."

Here's the thing nobody tells you. The ADHD brain runs on dopamine — and it's running a little low on it.

[B-ROLL]

So when a neurotypical brain looks at a boring task, it can go: "Eh, not fun, but I'll do it." Their brain fronts the motivation and collects the reward later.

Your brain? It wants the reward *first*. Or it's not moving.

Think of it like a taxi driver who refuses to turn the key until you pay upfront. The task offers no dopamine on the way in — so your brain just… idles. It's not that you don't care. Your engine literally won't start without fuel.

[B-ROLL]

That's why you can hyperfocus for six hours on something interesting, then be physically unable to send one text. Same brain. Different fuel levels.

So step one isn't "try harder." It's "make the boring thing give you something."

Which brings us to the actual negotiation tactics.

Section 2: Tactic One — Bribe Your Brain Upfront (Reward Stacking)

Since your brain wants payment before it works, give it payment before it works.

[B-ROLL]

This is called stacking. You pair the task you're avoiding with something that already gives you dopamine.

Real example: You've avoided doing dishes for three days. Don't just "do the dishes." Put on your comfort show. Grab your favorite drink. Now the dishes come with a soundtrack and a snack.

[B-ROLL]

Your brain isn't doing the dishes anymore. It's watching the show — and the dishes are just happening in the background. You tricked the taxi driver into thinking he already got paid.

The key: the reward has to happen *during* or *right before* the task. Not after. "I'll relax when I'm done" doesn't work, because your brain can't feel the future. It only responds to now.

[B-ROLL]

Bribe first. Work second. That's the deal your brain will actually sign.

Section 3: Tactic Two — Shrink the Task Until It's Embarrassingly Small

Here's the second thing dopamine and your brain argue about: the *size* of the task.

[B-ROLL]

In your head, "reply to that email" isn't one task. It's: find the email, remember the context, figure out what to say, worry it sounds wrong, imagine their response, panic. Your brain feels all of that at once. So it runs.

The fix is to make the first step so small it's almost stupid.

Don't "reply to the email." Just *open* the email. That's it. That's the whole task.

[B-ROLL]

Because here's the secret — starting *is* the hardest part. Once you're in motion, dopamine finally kicks in. The taxi's engine only needs the first turn of the key. After that, momentum carries you.

I call it the "two-minute door." You promise your brain you'll only do two minutes. And nine times out of ten, once you're through the door, you keep going. And the one time you don't? You still did two minutes. That's two minutes more than six days of nothing.

[B-ROLL]

Make it small enough that saying no feels ridiculous.

Section 4: Tactic Three — Add a Witness (Body Doubling)

The third negotiation trick is my favorite, because it barely feels like effort.

[B-ROLL]

It's called body doubling. You do your task while another person is present — either in the room or on a video call. They don't help. They don't even do the same thing. They just… exist near you while you work.

Sounds too simple. But it works because your brain treats a witness like accountability *and* a little hit of social dopamine. Suddenly the boring task has a reason to happen now.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like going to the gym. You'll skip your solo workout in a heartbeat. But when a friend is waiting for you? Somehow you show up. Same energy. A body double turns "I should" into "someone's here, might as well."

No friend available? There are entire online communities and apps where strangers just work quietly together on camera. It feels weird for about ninety seconds. Then it feels like a superpower.

[B-ROLL]

You're not weak for needing a witness. Your brain is just wired to respond to presence. Use it.

Section 5: Stop Punishing Yourself for the Six Days

Last one — and this is the one that actually changes everything.

[B-ROLL]

Here's what usually happens on day six. You finally think about the task, and your brain doesn't just feel the task anymore. It feels six days of guilt stacked on top of it. Shame. Frustration. "Why can't I just be normal."

And guess what shame does to a low-dopamine brain? It makes you avoid the task *even more*. Because now it hurts to look at.

[B-ROLL]

So the negotiation gets impossible. You're not fighting a four-minute task anymore. You're fighting an entire pile of self-blame.

The move here is boring but powerful: separate the shame from the task. The delay already happened. Beating yourself up doesn't undo day one through five. All it does is make day six harder.

[B-ROLL]

Try talking to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend. "Yeah, this took a while. That's okay. Let's just open it." That tiny bit of kindness lowers the pressure — and a lower-pressure task is a task your brain will actually approach.

Self-compassion isn't soft. It's strategy. Drop the shame, and you drop the biggest weight your brain was refusing to lift.
