After 2,000 Sleep Logs From Insomniacs, One Surprising Habit Kept Showing Up

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

I read 2,000 sleep logs.

Two thousand nights of someone lying awake, staring at the ceiling, begging their brain to shut up.

And I expected to find the usual suspects. Caffeine. Screens. Stress.

But that's not what showed up.

The pattern that kept appearing in almost every log had nothing to do with bedtime at all.

It happened the moment they woke up.

[B-ROLL]

And once you see it, you can't unsee it. So let me show you.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

If you have ADHD or an anxious brain, you already know the drill.

You get into bed exhausted. Genuinely exhausted. And then... your brain turns on.

Suddenly it wants to replay that awkward thing you said in 2014. It wants to plan your entire life. It wants to Google one small thing that becomes forty-five minutes.

[B-ROLL]

You've probably tried everything. The melatonin. The white noise. The "no screens an hour before bed" rule you broke in six minutes.

Here's the thing nobody told you.

Insomnia for a neurodivergent brain usually isn't a nighttime problem. It's a daytime problem that *shows up* at night.

I went through 2,000 sleep logs to find what the people who finally slept were actually doing differently.

And by the end of this video, you're going to walk away with one specific change you can make tomorrow morning. Not tonight. Tomorrow morning. That's the whole twist.

BODY

Section 1: The habit wasn't at bedtime — it was at wake-up

Here's what shocked me.

The people who slept well weren't doing anything fancy before bed. The habit that kept showing up happened within the first hour of being awake.

They got light. Real light. Outside light. Within about 30 to 60 minutes of waking.

[B-ROLL]

Think of your brain like a phone that needs to be plugged in to know what time it is. Morning light is the charger. It tells your internal clock, "Okay, the day starts now." And that quietly sets a timer for melatonin to release about 14 to 16 hours later.

Skip the light, and your brain genuinely doesn't know when night is supposed to be.

ADHD brains are especially sensitive to this because our circadian rhythm already tends to run late. We're the night owls by default. Morning light is the thing that drags the clock back into place.

[B-ROLL]

So the first pattern wasn't a sleeping habit. It was a waking habit. Now let me show you the second one — because this is where most people accidentally sabotage themselves.

Section 2: They stopped "catching up" on sleep

Almost every bad sleep log had the same quiet confession buried in it.

"Slept terribly, so I stayed in bed until 11."

[B-ROLL]

I get it. You slept badly. You're wrecked. Of course you want to grab more.

But here's the trap. When you sleep in to recover, you push your whole clock later. Which means that night, you're not tired at a normal time. Which means another bad night. Which means another late morning.

It's a loop. And the loop feeds itself.

Think of it like jet lag you give yourself every single weekend. You fly to a new time zone on Saturday and fly back on Monday, and your brain never lands.

[B-ROLL]

The people who slept well did something that felt almost cruel. They woke up at the same time. Even after a bad night. *Especially* after a bad night.

Not perfect. Not military. Just a consistent wake-up window, within about 30 minutes, most days.

Because here's the truth: your brain will forgive one tired day. What it can't handle is a clock that moves around constantly. Consistency is the medicine. The lie-in is the thing that keeps you sick.

[B-ROLL]

Okay. Light at the start. Fixed wake-up time. Now the third one — and this is the one that actually explains the racing thoughts.

Section 3: They had somewhere to put the brain noise

This is the one that matters most for ADHD and anxiety.

The racing thoughts at night aren't random. Your brain isn't broken. It's doing a job.

[B-ROLL]

All day, you're holding open loops. The email you didn't send. The appointment you need to book. The thing you promised someone. ADHD brains are terrible at letting go of open loops, because we don't trust ourselves to remember them later.

So guess when your brain decides to remind you of everything?

The second there's silence. Which is bedtime.

[B-ROLL]

The people who slept well had a place to dump it all. A notebook by the bed. A notes app. A voice memo. Some of them did a "brain dump" ten minutes before bed and just wrote down every open loop. Everything on the mental to-do list. No order. No judgment.

Here's why it works. Your brain keeps shouting because it's scared you'll forget. The moment you write it down, you're telling your brain, "It's handled. You can stand down."

Think of it like closing twenty browser tabs that were silently draining your battery.

[B-ROLL]

One person in the logs wrote: "I kept a pen by my bed for a week. First time in years I fell asleep without arguing with myself."

That line stuck with me.

So that's three. But there was one more thing I almost missed.

Section 4: They changed their relationship with being awake

This one is subtle. And it might be the most important.

The bad sleepers treated being awake at night like an emergency.

[B-ROLL]

You know the feeling. You wake up at 3 AM and immediately the panic starts. "Oh no, I'm awake, I have to sleep, it's 3, if I fall asleep now I'll get four hours, no three, oh god."

And the anxiety about not sleeping becomes the thing keeping you awake.

The good sleepers did something completely different. When they couldn't sleep, they didn't fight it. They got up. Went to another room. Did something boring and dim. A boring book. No bright screens. And they came back when they felt sleepy.

[B-ROLL]

Counterintuitive, right? But there's logic here. If you lie in bed awake and frustrated for an hour, your brain starts associating the bed with stress. The bed becomes the arena where you battle your own mind.

The fix is to make the bed boring again. Bed is for sleep. If you're not sleeping, you leave. You come back when sleepy. You retrain the association.

[B-ROLL]

It's not about forcing sleep. You can't force sleep. You can only remove the things fighting it. And the biggest thing fighting it is the pressure you put on yourself to do it right now.

So let me pull all of this together, because together it tells one story.

Section 5: The pattern underneath all of it

Here's what connects every single log.

The people who slept well stopped trying to *fix the night*. They fixed the *day* that led into the night.

[B-ROLL]

Morning light sets the clock. A fixed wake-up keeps the clock honest. A brain dump empties the open loops. And letting go of the pressure removes the fight.

Notice none of those happen when you're desperately trying to fall asleep.

That's the surprising habit. The thing that fixed the night was almost never done at night.

[B-ROLL]

If your brain runs hot — if you're the kind of person who feels most alive at midnight and most broken at 7 AM — this isn't a willpower problem. Your wiring just needs different support than the generic advice gives you.

And you can give it that support. One small change at a time.
