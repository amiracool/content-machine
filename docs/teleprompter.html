Trying to Sleep But Your Brain Has Autism 🧠🌙

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

It's 11 PM. You're exhausted. You've been tired all day.

You finally get into bed. The one thing you've been craving since you woke up.

And then... your brain goes, "Okay! Let's get started."

[B-ROLL]

Started on what? Replaying a conversation from 2014. Noticing the fridge is humming. Wondering if the fridge always hummed. Realizing your sock seam is touching your toe wrong.

If your body is tired but your brain refuses to sleep, this isn't insomnia being random. For a lot of autistic people, this is just how the brain works at night. And once you understand *why*, you can actually start fixing it.

Stay with me. This one's going to feel personal.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you.

Everyone treats sleep like it's a switch. You get in bed, you flip it off, you're out. And if you can't do that, people hand you the same tired advice. "Just put your phone down." "Try counting sheep." "Drink some tea."

[B-ROLL]

But for an autistic brain, sleep isn't a switch. It's a *landing*. And your brain is a plane that's still circling the runway at full speed, noticing every single light down below.

The problem isn't that you're lazy or bad at sleeping. The problem is that nobody designed "normal" bedtime for a nervous system like yours.

So in this video, I'm going to walk you through the four real reasons your autistic brain won't shut off at night — and exactly what to do about each one. No "just relax." Actual strategies that work *with* your brain instead of against it.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: Your sensory system never clocked out

During the day, you're filtering a flood of input. Lights, sounds, textures, conversations, temperature. All of it. Nonstop.

Most people's brains dim that input automatically. Autistic brains often don't. So you spend the whole day with the volume cranked to 100.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the trap. When you finally lie down, your brain doesn't go quiet. It goes, "Oh good, now I can finally *process* all of that." It's like you held a sneeze in all day and now it's happening at midnight.

Think of it like a dishwasher. You load it all day. The run cycle doesn't start until everything else is off. That loud grinding noise at 11 PM? That's your brain running the cycle you never got to during the day.

So what do you do?

You start the cycle *earlier*. Build in deliberate sensory downtime about an hour before bed. Not screen time. Actual decompression. Dim the lights way down. Lower the noise. Change into the softest clothes you own.

[B-ROLL]

You're not trying to force sleep. You're giving your sensory system the time it needs to finish processing — *before* your head hits the pillow.

But sensory overload is only part of it. Because even when the room is quiet...

Section 2: Your thoughts don't come with an off-ramp

This is the loop.

You lie down, and suddenly you're thinking about everything. And I mean *everything*. Did you lock the door. That weird thing you said. A documentary fact about octopuses. Whether octopuses sleep. Do they dream.

[B-ROLL]

For a lot of autistic people, the mind doesn't idle. It runs deep and it runs hard. During the day, tasks give those thoughts somewhere to go. At night, there's no task. So the thoughts just... pile up with nowhere to land.

It's like a browser with 47 tabs open and no way to close them. One of them is playing music and you can't find which one.

Here's what actually helps. You give your brain a container *outside* your head.

Keep a notebook by the bed. When a thought shows up, write it down. Not to solve it. Just to park it. The thought loops partly because your brain is scared it'll forget something important. Writing it down tells your brain, "It's handled. You can let go now."

[B-ROLL]

And if your mind needs something to focus on, give it a *boring* loop on purpose. A podcast you've heard ten times. An audiobook you know by heart. Autistic brains often sleep better with a gentle, predictable focus than with total silence. Silence just leaves room for the 47 tabs.

Okay. But sometimes it's not the thoughts and it's not the lights. Sometimes it's your actual body.

Section 3: Your body and your sleep clock aren't on speaking terms

Here's something a lot of autistic people don't find out until adulthood.

Research shows autistic people often have differences in melatonin — the hormone that tells your body it's night. For some, it releases late. For some, it's lower than usual. Which means your body might genuinely not get the "it's bedtime" memo on schedule.

[B-ROLL]

So you're lying there at midnight feeling weirdly alert, and you think something's wrong with you. There isn't. Your internal clock is just running on a different timezone than the rest of the world.

Think of it like jet lag you never earned. You didn't fly anywhere. Your body just decided it's in a different time zone and forgot to tell you.

A few things help here, and they're not complicated.

Get bright light in your eyes early in the day. Real daylight if you can. Morning light is one of the strongest signals for resetting your clock.

[B-ROLL]

Then dim the world down at night. Warm lights, lower screens, maybe blue-light filters. You're sending your body a clear sunrise-and-sunset signal it might not be generating on its own.

And if you've tried all of this and your clock is still completely off — talk to a doctor about melatonin. For autistic people specifically, it can be a real difference-maker. But get the dose and timing checked, because more is not better. This isn't medical advice, it's a nudge to ask the right question.

Now there's one last piece. And honestly, this might be the most important one.

Section 4: You've been fighting your brain instead of working with it

Here's the pattern I see over and over.

You try the thing. It doesn't work instantly. So you lie there getting angry at yourself for not sleeping. And now you're stressed about being stressed. Which is the single best way to guarantee you stay awake.

[B-ROLL]

The anxiety of *not sleeping* becomes a bigger problem than the original difficulty. You've turned your bed into a battleground. And your brain remembers that. So now just lying down makes you tense.

Here's the shift. Stop trying to *force* sleep. Your job at night isn't to fall asleep. It's just to rest. That's it.

Take the pressure completely off. Tell yourself, "I don't have to sleep. I just have to lie here and be comfortable." Sounds too simple. But when you remove the demand, your nervous system finally relaxes. And relaxing is the actual doorway to sleep.

[B-ROLL]

And build a routine that's the *same every single night*. Autistic brains love predictability. Same order, same steps, same wind-down. Your brain starts to recognize the sequence and goes, "Ah. This again. This means sleep is coming." You're not forcing it. You're teaching it.

That's the whole secret. You stop treating your brain like it's broken. And you start giving it what it was always asking for.
