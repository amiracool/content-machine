My Response to the Wellness Influencer Telling You to Quit Your Meds and "Heal Naturally"

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

There's a video going viral right now.

A wellness influencer — glowing skin, perfect lighting, a smoothie in hand — looks straight into the camera and says: "Throw away your ADHD meds. Heal naturally. Your body already knows how."

That video has millions of views.

And I need to tell you something before you take that advice.

Because I almost did. And it nearly cost me my job, my relationship, and honestly? My sense of who I am.

[B-ROLL]

So let's talk about what she got dangerously wrong.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

If you're watching this, I already know something about you.

You're neurodivergent. You've maybe just started medication — or you're thinking about it. And you feel *guilty* about it.

Because everywhere you look, someone's telling you that pills are "the easy way out." That if you just drank more water, meditated harder, cut out sugar, and "healed your gut," you wouldn't need them.

And that guilt? It's loud. It follows you into the pharmacy.

[B-ROLL]

Here's what I want to give you in the next few minutes.

Not a lecture. Not "medication is the only answer." But the actual truth about what these influencers are selling — and how to make this decision from a place of facts instead of fear.

By the end, you'll know exactly how to tell real support from someone monetising your insecurity.

Let's get into it.

---

BODY

Section 1: "Natural" is a marketing word, not a medical one

Here's the first thing nobody tells you.

The word "natural" has no medical meaning. None. It's a *feeling* they're selling you, not a treatment.

Think about it this way. Poison ivy is natural. Nightshade is natural. A broken bone healing crooked because you refused a cast — that's also "natural."

[B-ROLL]

Nobody tells a diabetic to "heal their insulin naturally." Nobody tells someone with poor eyesight that glasses are "the easy way out."

But with ADHD — with *brains* — suddenly everyone's an expert. Suddenly needing support is a personal failing.

The influencer isn't offering you science. She's offering you a story where you're the hero who didn't need help. And that story sells supplements.

Which brings me to the next thing.

[B-ROLL]

Section 2: Follow the money — because it's always there

I want you to do one thing next time you see one of these videos.

Look at the caption. Look at the pinned comment. Look at the "link in bio."

Almost every single time, there's a product. A supplement subscription. A $300 "brain reset" course. A discount code.

[B-ROLL]

That's not a coincidence. That's the whole business model.

Here's the uncomfortable part. When a psychiatrist prescribes you medication, they don't get richer if you take more of it. There are ethics boards. Regulations. Follow-up appointments.

When an influencer tells you to quit your meds, they profit *directly* from your doubt. Your fear is their conversion rate.

[B-ROLL]

I'm not saying every supplement is a scam. I'm saying: be as skeptical of the person selling you a cure as you'd be of anyone selling you anything.

Real professionals want you stable. Not subscribed.

Section 3: What actually happened when I tried "healing naturally"

Let me tell you my story. Because I did it. I quit cold turkey.

I watched enough of these videos that I convinced myself I was "stronger than a pill." I threw them out. I bought the adaptogens. I did the cold plunges. I journaled at 5am.

[B-ROLL]

For about a week, I felt amazing. Empowered. In control.

Then the crash came.

I missed three deadlines. I forgot my best friend's birthday. I sat in my car outside work for forty minutes because I couldn't make my brain start the day. I snapped at people I love.

[B-ROLL]

The cold plunges didn't fix my working memory. The smoothies didn't help me finish a task. Because ADHD isn't a wellness deficiency. It's a difference in how my brain manages attention and dopamine.

Here's what I learned the hard way. Meditation is *wonderful*. Sleep matters. Exercise genuinely helps. Nutrition supports your brain.

But they're the seatbelt — not the engine.

[B-ROLL]

For a lot of us, medication is what gets the engine running so all the other stuff can *actually work.*

Section 4: The dangerous part nobody's talking about

Now here's where it goes past bad advice and into genuinely harmful.

Telling someone to quit psychiatric medication cold turkey — with no doctor, no taper, no plan — can be dangerous.

[B-ROLL]

Some medications need to be reduced gradually. Stopping suddenly can cause your symptoms to come roaring back — sometimes worse than before. For some people, unmanaged ADHD raises real risks: car accidents, job loss, impulsive decisions, spiralling mental health.

The influencer won't be there for any of that. She's already scrolling to her next video.

She has zero medical training, zero knowledge of your history, zero liability. And she's giving medical advice to millions of strangers.

[B-ROLL]

That's not wellness. That's just recklessness with good lighting.

If you want to change anything about your medication — and that's completely valid — you do it *with* a professional. Not because of a stranger's smoothie.

Section 5: How to actually tell good advice from garbage

So let me give you the filter I use now. Three quick questions.

One. Are they selling something? If the advice comes with a discount code, be extra careful.

[B-ROLL]

Two. Are they making a blanket statement for millions of different brains? "Everyone should quit their meds" is a red flag. Real support is individual. Your brain isn't a trend.

Three. Do they respect your autonomy — or shame you into a decision? Good advice gives you options. Manipulation gives you guilt.

[B-ROLL]

If a piece of content leaves you feeling ashamed for getting help — that's not health advice. That's just marketing wearing a wellness costume.

You are allowed to take your medication. You are allowed to *not.* But you get to make that choice with facts, with a doctor, and without a stranger's guilt trip in your ear.

[B-ROLL]

Needing help isn't weakness. It never was. The bravest thing I ever did wasn't throwing out my meds.

It was walking back into that doctor's office and saying, "I think I made a mistake."
