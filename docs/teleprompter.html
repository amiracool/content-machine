My Response to the Doctor Who Told Me ADHD "Isn't Real" in Adults

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

A doctor once looked me dead in the eye and said, "ADHD isn't real in adults. You just need to try harder."

I was 31. I'd spent my whole life trying harder. And it never worked.

[B-ROLL]

So today, I'm giving him the response I couldn't give in that room. Because if a doctor said this to you — or if a version of that voice lives in your head right now — I need you to hear what I'm about to say.

This isn't a rant. It's the truth I wish someone had handed me a decade ago.

[B-ROLL]

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you're probably in.

You suspect you have ADHD. Maybe you've suspected it for years. You've done the online quizzes at 2am. You've watched the videos. You've felt that jolt of *that's me* over and over again.

[B-ROLL]

But then you finally worked up the courage to ask a professional. And someone — a doctor, a family member, a "well-meaning" friend — waved it off.

"Everyone's a little ADHD these days." "You went to university, so you can't have it." "That's just an excuse."

[B-ROLL]

And now you're stuck. Doubting yourself. Wondering if you're broken or just lazy.

So here's what I'm going to give you in the next few minutes. I'm going to break down exactly why that doctor was wrong. I'll show you the science he ignored, the reason so many adults slip through the cracks, and how to move forward — even when the people who are supposed to help you shut the door.

By the end, you'll have language for what you've been feeling. And you'll stop waiting for permission.

BODY

Section 1: Adult ADHD isn't a trend — it's a diagnosis we missed the first time

Here's the thing that doctor got backwards.

Adults with ADHD didn't suddenly develop it. We were kids with ADHD who nobody caught.

[B-ROLL]

Think about it. The image of ADHD used to be one thing only — a hyperactive little boy bouncing off the walls. So if you were a quiet kid staring out the window, or a girl who daydreamed, or someone who was "smart but scattered," you got missed. Completely.

It's like there was a net designed to catch one type of fish. And everyone else swam straight through.

[B-ROLL]

So when a doctor says adult ADHD isn't real, what he's actually saying is: "Because we failed to diagnose you as a child, you don't get to have it now." That's not science. That's just doubling down on the original mistake.

And the research backs this up. ADHD is one of the most well-studied conditions in psychiatry. It shows up on brain imaging. It has a strong genetic link. It doesn't vanish at 18.

Which brings me to the next thing he got wrong.

Section 2: "You're functioning, so you're fine" is a trap

The doctor looked at my life and saw a job, a degree, sentences that made sense. And he decided I was fine.

[B-ROLL]

But here's what he didn't see.

He didn't see the three alarms I need to wake up. The projects I finished at 4am because I couldn't start them until panic forced me. The friendships I lost because I forgot to reply for six weeks. The shame spiral every single time.

[B-ROLL]

This is what nobody tells you about high-functioning ADHD. The functioning is the cost. It's not proof you're okay. It's proof of how hard you're paddling under the water while everyone thinks you're gliding.

It's like judging whether a swimmer is drowning by whether they're still above the surface. Sometimes the ones fighting hardest look the calmest — right until they go under.

[B-ROLL]

So when someone says "you're too successful to have ADHD," flip it. Ask them how much of your energy that success is quietly eating. Ask them what it's costing you.

Because coping is not the same as thriving. And surviving your own brain shouldn't be a full-time job.

Section 3: "Just try harder" is the most damaging advice you can give

This is the part that actually made me angry.

"Try harder."

[B-ROLL]

Do you know how many times an ADHD brain has already tried harder? We are the try-harder champions of the world. We've built entire personalities around overcompensating. Around apologizing. Around white-knuckling through tasks that other people just... do.

ADHD isn't a motivation problem. It's a regulation problem. The issue was never *wanting* to do the thing. The issue is the bridge between wanting and doing.

[B-ROLL]

Telling someone with ADHD to try harder is like telling someone with bad eyesight to squint harder. Sure, they can strain themselves. But you're ignoring the fact that they need glasses. The glasses aren't a crutch. They're the fix.

[B-ROLL]

And here's the cruel part. When "try harder" doesn't work — because it can't — we don't blame the advice. We blame ourselves. That's where the anxiety comes from. The depression. The belief that we're just fundamentally lazy or broken.

That doctor didn't just give me bad advice. He handed me another decade of self-blame. And I refuse to let anyone else pick that up and carry it.

Section 4: You don't need one gatekeeper's permission to understand yourself

Here's what I wish I'd known walking out of that office.

One person's opinion is not a verdict on your entire brain.

[B-ROLL]

That doctor wasn't a specialist in adult ADHD. He'd read a headline, maybe, and formed a bias. But I let his three-minute judgment override thirty years of my own lived experience. That was the mistake.

So if you've been dismissed, here's what you do. You get a second opinion. You look for a professional who actually specializes in adult neurodiversity — not a generalist who's still working off the outdated playbook.

[B-ROLL]

You document your patterns. Write down the daily stuff. The lost keys, the interrupted conversations, the time blindness, the way your brain has forty tabs open and none of them are the one playing music.

[B-ROLL]

Because when you walk in with evidence, it's a lot harder to wave you away. And even before any official label — understanding *why* your brain works the way it does is already healing. The label is helpful. But the self-compassion is the medicine.

You are allowed to seek answers. You are allowed to trust your own experience. And you are absolutely allowed to walk away from anyone who makes you feel small for asking.

Section 5: What I'd actually say to him now

So, doctor, if you're watching. Here's my real response.

[B-ROLL]

You were wrong. Not a little wrong — dangerously wrong. And you weren't just wrong about me. You were wrong about the thousands of adults who walk into offices like yours every week, terrified, holding onto their last shred of hope that someone will finally take them seriously.

Your job wasn't to protect an outdated idea of what ADHD looks like. Your job was to *see* people. And you didn't see me.

But here's the thing. I don't need your validation anymore. I got the assessment. I got the answers. And for the first time in my life, I stopped hating myself for being the way I am.

[B-ROLL]

So thank you, actually. Because that awful moment lit a fire that led me here. To this. To telling everyone watching that they are not broken, not lazy, and not making it up.

Your brain is not a character flaw. It's just wired differently. And different is not less.

[B-ROLL]

And right now, go watch my video on the top signs of ADHD in adults that everyone overlooks. It's the exact list I wish I'd had before that appointment. It might be the thing that finally makes it all click.

[B-ROLL]

You deserve answers. So go get them.

I'll see you in the next one.
