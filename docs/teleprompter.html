BODY

Section 1: ADHD Paralysis Is a Traffic Jam, Not a Dead Battery

Here's the first thing nobody tells you. ADHD paralysis isn't a lack of energy. It's a *backup* of energy with nowhere to go.

Think of your brain like a highway. In a non-ADHD brain, tasks flow through one at a time. Nice and orderly. One car, then the next.

In an ADHD brain? Every task shows up at once, honking, trying to merge into the same lane. The email. The dishes. That text you forgot to answer. The thing from three weeks ago. They all arrive at the same second.

[B-ROLL]

And when everything feels equally urgent, your brain does the only thing it can to protect itself. It shuts the whole highway down.

That's the freeze. That's the paralysis.

So when people say "just do one thing," they don't understand that your brain genuinely cannot see which thing is "one thing." They're all screaming at the same volume.

Once I understood this, I stopped blaming myself. I wasn't out of gas. I was in gridlock. And gridlock needs a *different* solution than a dead battery.

[B-ROLL]

Which brings me to the first thing that actually clears the jam.

Section 2: Shrink the Task Until It Feels Almost Stupid

The number one mistake? We pick a task that's *way* too big for a stuck brain.

"Clean the kitchen" is not a task. It's a project. It's got forty hidden steps your brain can already see — the dishes, the counters, the trash, the floor — and that's the overload.

[B-ROLL]

So here's the fix. Shrink the task until it feels almost embarrassingly small. Not "clean the kitchen." Not even "do the dishes."

*Pick up one fork.*

That's it. One fork.

Because here's what happens. The hardest part of any task for an ADHD brain is *starting*. It's the activation energy. Once you're in motion, momentum takes over. That's basic physics — an object in motion stays in motion.

[B-ROLL]

I call it the "ridiculously small first step." Make it so tiny that your brain can't argue with it. Your brain *wants* to protest "that's not enough." Good. Let it protest. You've already started.

Nine times out of ten, that one fork turns into the whole kitchen. And the one time it doesn't? You still did *one fork.* That's one fork more than paralysis.

[B-ROLL]

But sometimes the task isn't the problem. Sometimes it's the *feeling* underneath it. Let's talk about that.

Section 3: Name the Emotion Hiding Under the Task

This one blew my mind.

A lot of ADHD paralysis isn't about the task at all. It's about an *emotion* glued to the task that you haven't noticed.

[B-ROLL]

That email you can't send? Maybe it's not hard to write. Maybe you're scared of how the person will respond. That's anxiety, not laziness.

That project you keep avoiding? Maybe it's attached to a fear of failing. Or a memory of being criticized. Or the perfectionism that whispers "if you can't do it perfectly, don't do it at all."

ADHD brains feel emotions *intensely.* We don't get a gentle nudge of dread. We get a flood. And the brain freezes to avoid the flood.

So try this. When you're stuck, stop and ask: *"What am I actually feeling right now?"*

Not "why am I so lazy." That question is a trap. Ask: "What emotion is under this?"

[B-ROLL]

Sometimes just *naming* it — "oh, I'm scared this will be bad" — releases enough pressure for the highway to open back up. Psychologists literally call this "name it to tame it." You bring the feeling out of the fog, and it loses its grip.

You're not avoiding the dishes. You're avoiding a feeling. Find the feeling, and the dishes get a lot easier.

[B-ROLL]

Now — what about those days when even the tiny step feels impossible?

Section 4: Borrow a Brain (The Power of Body Doubling)

On the worst days, nothing internal works. And that's not a failure. That's when you reach *outside* yourself.

It's called body doubling. And it sounds too simple to work. But it's one of the most powerful ADHD tools that exists.

[B-ROLL]

Body doubling just means doing your task while another person is present. They don't help you. They don't even have to do the same thing. They just *exist* in the room while you work.

Why does this work? Because the ADHD brain regulates better with external accountability. Another person's presence gives your brain a point of focus — an anchor. It's like your attention finally has somewhere to park.

[B-ROLL]

Can't get someone in the room? Hop on a video call and mute yourselves. Join a virtual focus session online. Even putting on a livestream of someone studying can trick your brain into co-working mode.

I've cleaned my whole apartment on a silent video call with a friend who was doing her taxes. Neither of us said a word. Both of us got unstuck.

You are not meant to do everything alone in your own head. Your brain was *built* to work better alongside others. Use that.

[B-ROLL]

And there's one more thing — the piece that holds all of this together.

Section 5: Drop the Guilt, Because Shame Is the Glue That Keeps You Stuck

Here's the hard one.

Every strategy I just gave you will fail if you keep drowning in shame.

Because shame *is* paralysis fuel. The more you beat yourself up for being stuck, the more your nervous system floods, and the deeper the freeze goes. You can't shame your way out of a shame spiral. It's like pouring gasoline on a fire to put it out.

[B-ROLL]

So I want you to try a different voice. When you catch yourself frozen, instead of "what is wrong with me," try: *"My brain is overloaded right now. That's okay. What's one tiny thing?"*

That's not fluffy self-help. That's harm reduction. A calm nervous system can move. A panicked one cannot.

[B-ROLL]

You didn't choose this wiring. You're not lazy. You're not broken. You've got a brain that processes the world at a different speed — and it comes with real struggles *and* real strengths.

The goal was never to become a productivity robot. The goal is to work *with* your brain, treat yourself like someone you actually care about, and get unstuck a little faster each time.

That's it. That's the whole game.
