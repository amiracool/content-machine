When ADHD People Try to "Just Start" a Task 🤣

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When ADHD people try to "just start" a task they've been dreading for three weeks...

[B-ROLL]

Here's what nobody tells you.

It's not that we're lazy. It's not that we don't care.

It's that "just start" is the four most useless words you can say to an ADHD brain.

[B-ROLL]

And by the end of this video, you're gonna understand exactly what's happening in your brain when you can't begin a simple task. Plus one trick that actually works.

Stick with me.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Okay so let's talk about it.

You have a task. It's not even hard. Maybe it's replying to one email. Maybe it's booking a doctor's appointment. Maybe it's folding a single basket of laundry.

[B-ROLL]

And you've been meaning to do it for... three weeks. Maybe three months.

Every day you tell yourself, "Today's the day. I'll just do it."

[B-ROLL]

And then the day ends. And it's still not done. And now you're carrying this weird guilt around like a backpack full of bricks.

Sound familiar?

Here's the thing. The problem was never the task. The problem is that neurotypical productivity advice was designed for a brain that isn't yours.

So in this video, I'm gonna break down the three real reasons your brain won't let you start. And I'm gonna give you a way in that doesn't require willpower you don't have.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Your Brain Doesn't Run on Importance. It Runs on Interest.

Here's the truth that changed everything for me.

The ADHD brain isn't motivated by how *important* something is. It's motivated by interest, novelty, challenge, or urgency.

[B-ROLL]

So when you tell yourself, "This is important, I should do it," your brain goes... "Cool story. Still not interested."

Think of it like a car that only runs on a very specific type of fuel. You can stand there yelling "GO!" at the gas tank all day. But if you're pouring in the wrong fuel, nothing moves.

[B-ROLL]

That's why you can clean your entire kitchen at 11pm the night before a deadline, but you can't fold laundry at 2pm on a free Saturday. The urgency wasn't there. So the fuel wasn't there.

This isn't a character flaw. It's literally how your dopamine system works.

So the first shift is this: stop waiting to *feel* motivated. Motivation isn't the starting line for us. It usually shows up *after* we've started.

Which brings me to the actual reason you can't start...

Section 2: The Task Isn't One Task. It's Twelve Invisible Tasks.

When a neurotypical person hears "book a doctor's appointment," they hear one task.

When you hear it, your brain silently unpacks the whole thing.

[B-ROLL]

Find the number. Wait, which doctor? Do I need a referral? What if they ask questions I can't answer? What time works? What if I have to call and I hate calling? What if there's hold music? What day is even good? What was I doing again?

[B-ROLL]

So it's not that you're avoiding *one* small thing. Your brain is looking at a mountain of hidden steps, and it has no idea where the trailhead even is.

This is called task paralysis. And it's not drama. It's your working memory getting flooded before you've even moved a muscle.

Here's the analogy. Imagine someone hands you a thousand-piece puzzle with no picture on the box. And they go, "Just start."

[B-ROLL]

Start *where*? That's the whole problem.

So the fix isn't to try harder. The fix is to make the task ridiculously, almost insultingly small. So small it feels stupid.

Don't "book the appointment." Just "open your phone and find the number." That's it. That's the whole task.

[B-ROLL]

Because once you find the number, you're already moving. And a moving ADHD brain is way easier to work with than a frozen one.

Section 3: Starting Feels Physically Uncomfortable — And That's Real.

Okay this is the part I wish someone told me years ago.

For a lot of us, starting a boring task doesn't just feel annoying. It feels almost *physically* bad. Like there's an invisible wall between you and the thing.

[B-ROLL]

You want to move toward it. But something in your body pulls you the other way. And so you end up doing anything else. Suddenly the fridge needs organizing. Suddenly you're researching the history of a country you'll never visit.

[B-ROLL]

That's not procrastination in the lazy sense. That's your brain seeking a dopamine hit anywhere *except* the uncomfortable task.

Here's a way to think about it. Your brain is trying to protect you from discomfort, but it's using the worst possible strategy. It's like refusing to jump into a cold pool, so instead you just... stand at the edge for two hours, cold and miserable anyway.

[B-ROLL]

The jump was always the easy part. The standing there was the torture.

So the trick here is to shrink the discomfort window. Tell yourself you only have to do the thing for five minutes. Set a timer. When it goes off, you're genuinely allowed to stop.

[B-ROLL]

Most of the time? You won't stop. Because you've already broken through the wall. The hardest part — the first sixty seconds — is behind you.

Section 4: Body Doubling Is a Cheat Code.

Now let me give you the one thing that works when nothing else does.

It's called body doubling. And it sounds too simple to work, but it's borderline magic.

[B-ROLL]

Body doubling just means doing your task while another person is present. They don't have to help. They don't even have to be doing the same thing. They just have to *be there*.

Think about it. How many times have you cleaned your whole house because a friend said they were coming over?

[B-ROLL]

That's not a coincidence. The presence of another person creates just enough gentle accountability and just enough novelty to unstick your brain.

No friend around? Hop on a video call and both work silently. Or put on a "study with me" video. Or literally text someone, "I'm starting my laundry now, hold me to it."

[B-ROLL]

External structure isn't a weakness. Your brain struggles to generate its own structure, so you borrow it from the outside. That's not cheating. That's working *with* your wiring instead of against it.

Section 5: Ditch the Guilt — It's Making Everything Worse.

Last one. And it might be the most important.

Every time you fail to start a task, you pile on guilt. And that guilt doesn't motivate you. It does the opposite.

[B-ROLL]

Because now the task isn't just "fold laundry." The task is "fold laundry AND face the fact that I've failed to fold laundry for three weeks AND feel like something's wrong with me."

[B-ROLL]

That emotional weight makes the task feel even heavier. So you avoid it more. Which creates more guilt. It's a loop.

Here's the reframe. You didn't fail. Your strategy just didn't match your brain. Those are completely different things.

[B-ROLL]

When you drop the shame and treat this like a puzzle to solve instead of a moral failure, everything gets lighter. And a lighter task is one you can actually approach.

So next time you're frozen? Try talking to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend. Not "why can't you just do this," but "okay, what's the tiniest first step?"

That one change in tone can be the difference between another lost day and finally getting started.
