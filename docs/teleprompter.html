After 10 Years Treating OCD, I Realized Everyone Misunderstands the Exact Same Thing

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

After ten years treating OCD, I realized almost everyone misunderstands the exact same thing.

And I mean everyone. Patients. Parents. Teachers. Even some therapists.

Here's the truth that took me a decade to fully see: OCD was never really about germs. Or locks. Or counting. Or symmetry.

Those are the symptoms people show you. But the actual engine underneath? Nobody talks about it.

And once you understand it, OCD stops looking like a quirky "cleanliness thing" and starts making heartbreaking, perfect sense.

[B-ROLL]

Stick with me. Because this one shift changes how you treat it, how you support someone with it, and how you talk to yourself.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Let me guess why you're here.

Maybe you have intrusive thoughts that disgust you. Thoughts you'd never act on, but they loop anyway. And you're terrified that having them means something is wrong with you.

[B-ROLL]

Or maybe you love someone with OCD. You've watched them check the stove six times. You've said "just stop" — and watched it make everything worse. And you feel helpless.

Here's your exact problem: you've been treating the *behavior* instead of the *engine*. You've been fighting the smoke instead of the fire.

In the next few minutes, I'm going to show you the one thing underneath every type of OCD. The thing that connects the hand-washer, the counter, the "what if I'm a bad person" sufferer, and the person who can't leave the house until the words feel "right."

And by the end, you'll understand why the standard advice — "just relax, it's not a big deal" — is the worst thing you can say.

BODY

Section 1: OCD is not about the thing. It's about uncertainty.

Here's the pattern I saw across hundreds of people. The specific obsession changes. The underlying intolerance of uncertainty does not.

Think about it. The person checking the lock isn't in love with locks. They can't tolerate the *possibility* that something bad might happen and it'd be their fault. The lock is just where the uncertainty landed that day.

[B-ROLL]

I had a patient once who was terrified of harming someone. Kind, gentle, wouldn't hurt a fly. But OCD doesn't attack what you don't care about. It attacks exactly what you value most — then demands 100% certainty that you'd never cross that line.

And certainty like that doesn't exist. For anyone. That's the trap.

So they check. They reassure. They avoid. Trying to buy a feeling of "I know for sure." And it never comes.

[B-ROLL]

Once you see OCD as a disorder of *doubt*, not a disorder of *dirt*, everything reorganizes.

Let me show you what that doubt actually feels like.

Section 2: The compulsion isn't the problem. It's the failed solution.

Everyone focuses on the compulsion. The washing. The checking. The counting.

But here's what I misunderstood for years too: the compulsion is the person's *attempt to cope.* It's not the disease. It's the medicine that stopped working.

[B-ROLL]

Think of an itch. A mosquito bite. You scratch it, and for three seconds — relief. Pure relief.

But then it itches worse. So you scratch harder. And now it's bleeding.

That's a compulsion. The relief is real but tiny and temporary. And every scratch teaches your brain, "See? That worked." So next time the urge is stronger.

[B-ROLL]

This is why "just stop doing it" fails so badly. You're asking someone to let the itch burn without scratching. Their whole body is screaming to make it stop.

The goal isn't to never feel the urge. The goal is to feel the urge — and not scratch. To let the discomfort rise, peak, and fall on its own.

That's the actual treatment. And it has a name.

Section 3: Recovery feels backwards — on purpose.

The gold-standard treatment for OCD is called ERP. Exposure and Response Prevention.

And it sounds almost cruel until you understand it. You deliberately face the fear, and you deliberately *don't* do the compulsion.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the analogy I use. Imagine you're scared of a dog that barks every time you walk past. So you cross the street to avoid it. Every day, you cross. The fear gets bigger.

ERP is walking up to the fence. Letting the dog bark. And staying. Not running.

And here's the magic. After a few minutes, the dog gets bored. The barking slows. Your heart rate drops. And your brain learns something it could never learn by running: *I can handle this. The fear passes on its own.*

[B-ROLL]

That's recovery. Not the absence of fear. The discovery that you can tolerate it.

But I need to warn you about the thing people get wrong even inside treatment.

Section 4: Reassurance is a compulsion in disguise.

This is the one almost nobody catches.

When someone with OCD asks, "Are you sure I locked the door? Are you sure I'm not a bad person?" — and you answer — you think you're helping.

You're not. You're scratching the itch *for* them.

[B-ROLL]

Reassurance feels like love. But to OCD, it's fuel. It confirms the thought was worth checking. And ten minutes later, the doubt comes back, louder, asking the same question.

I've watched families spend years trapped in this loop. Hours a day answering the same question. Exhausted. And wondering why nothing improves.

The hardest, most loving thing you can learn to say is: "I can't answer that, because I love you and I don't want to feed it."

[B-ROLL]

It feels mean. It's actually the opposite. You're refusing to reinforce the prison.

And that leads to the biggest misunderstanding of all.

Section 5: The thought is not the person.

Here's the thing I wish I could tattoo onto every anxious brain.

Having a thought does not mean you want it. Does not mean it's true. Does not mean it reflects who you are.

[B-ROLL]

The person tormented by a violent thought is tormented *precisely because* they'd never do it. The person afraid they're secretly a bad person is usually one of the most conscientious people you'll meet.

OCD weaponizes your conscience. It takes your deepest values and says, "But what if?"

And the brain, trying to protect you, treats that thought like a threat. Analyzes it. Checks it. Loops it.

[B-ROLL]

But a thought is just mental weather. It arrives. It passes. You don't have to grab every one and interrogate it.

The sufferers who recover aren't the ones who finally prove the thought wrong. They're the ones who learn to say, "Huh. Weird thought," and let it float by without chasing it.

That's the whole shift. From "I must solve this doubt" to "I can live alongside uncertainty."

And that's the thing everyone misunderstands. OCD isn't about being clean, or careful, or perfect. It's about a brain that can't sit with *not knowing* — and a set of exhausting rituals built to escape a feeling that only grows when you run.

Because you are not your thoughts. You never were.
