POV: Telling Someone With Rejection Sensitivity "We Need to Talk" 😭

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"Hey... we need to talk."

Four words. That's all it took.

[B-ROLL]

And now their entire nervous system is on fire.

You meant "can you grab milk on the way home." Their brain heard "this relationship is over and everyone secretly hates you."

If you've ever sent that text and gotten a paragraph back that felt WILDLY out of proportion... stay right here.

Because I'm about to show you what actually happens inside the brain of someone with rejection sensitivity in the sixty seconds after you hit send.

It's not drama. It's not being "too sensitive." It's neurology. And once you see it, you'll never send that text the same way again.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

Here's the thing nobody tells you.

For a lot of neurodivergent people — especially folks with ADHD — rejection doesn't feel like a small "ouch." It feels like a physical injury. There's even a name for it: Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria. RSD.

And "we need to talk" is basically the nuclear button.

You're not doing anything wrong by wanting to talk. That's normal. Healthy, even.

The problem is the gap. The gap between sending that message and actually having the conversation. In that gap, an RSD brain will write, direct, and star in the worst movie you've ever seen.

So here's what I'm going to do for you.

I'm going to walk you through exactly what's happening on both sides — the sender AND the receiver. And by the end, I'll give you the one sentence that defuses the whole thing. A tiny change that takes two seconds and saves the other person from an hour of silent panic.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: The Text Lands Like a Bomb

The moment those four words arrive, the brain skips straight past logic and hits full threat mode.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the analogy. Imagine your smoke alarm went off in the middle of the night. You don't calmly think, "I wonder if this is a real fire or just burnt toast." Your body reacts FIRST. Heart pounding. Adrenaline dumping. You're up and moving before your brain catches up.

That's what "we need to talk" does to an RSD nervous system. The threat response fires before the thinking part of the brain even gets a vote.

[B-ROLL]

So when the other person reads it calmly and thinks "why is this a big deal" — remember, they're not reading the same message. Their body already sounded the alarm.

Now watch what the alarm makes them do next.

Section 2: The Brain Starts Writing Fan Fiction

This is where it gets wild. With no information to go on, the RSD brain doesn't sit and wait patiently. It fills the silence. And it fills it with the worst possible story.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the concrete example. You send "we need to talk" and then you go make dinner. Totally fine. You'll bring it up in an hour.

But in that hour, they've mentally reviewed every interaction from the last three weeks. That joke you didn't laugh at. The text you left on read. The time you said "it's fine" a little too fast.

[B-ROLL]

Every one of those becomes "proof." Their brain is a detective who's already decided you're guilty and is just collecting evidence.

And here's the cruel part — they KNOW it's probably not that deep. They'll tell themselves "you're overreacting." But knowing it and feeling it are two completely different systems. You can't logic your way out of an alarm that's already blaring.

So while you're seasoning the chicken, they're rehearsing their goodbye speech.

Section 3: The Physical Symptoms Nobody Sees

Rejection sensitivity isn't just an emotion. It shows up in the body. And most people have no idea it's happening.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like an allergic reaction, but to the FEELING of being rejected. Some people get hives from peanuts. RSD brains get a full stress response from perceived rejection.

The chest gets tight. The stomach drops. Some people feel nauseous. Some can't sit still. Some go completely numb and quiet — which, ironically, makes them look "fine" when they're anything but.

[B-ROLL]

So if you eventually walk in to talk and they seem tense, shut down, or weirdly defensive before you've even said anything — that's not attitude. That's a body that's been in fight-or-flight for the last sixty minutes.

They didn't get to skip the panic. They lived in it while you were cooking.

Which brings us to the moment that makes or breaks everything.

Section 4: The Conversation Itself

Here's what most people get wrong. They think the hard part is the actual talk. It's not. The hard part already happened — it was the waiting.

[B-ROLL]

So when you finally say "hey, I just wanted to figure out the weekend plans"... watch their face. There's this whole-body deflation of relief.

[B-ROLL]

And sometimes? They might even seem annoyed. Like, "THAT'S what this was about??" That's not ingratitude. That's the crash after an adrenaline high. Their body prepared for a war that never came, and now all that energy has nowhere to go.

The analogy here is bracing for a punch that never lands. You still flinched. Your muscles still tensed. The relief doesn't erase the exhaustion.

So the goal isn't to walk on eggshells forever. The goal is to close the gap. And that's the fix.

Section 5: The Two-Second Fix

Here it is. The whole thing you came for.

Don't send "we need to talk" and then disappear. Send the topic WITH it.

[B-ROLL]

That's it. Add the subject line. Add "nothing bad." Add the smiley if you want. Give the brain something to hold onto so it can't write its own horror story.

Because remember — the panic isn't caused by the conversation. It's caused by the UNKNOWN. You're not being asked to over-explain or coddle anyone. You're just refusing to leave a blank space for their brain to fill with nightmares.

[B-ROLL]

And if you're the one WITH rejection sensitivity? Here's your move. You're allowed to reply, "totally fine — quick question, is everything okay?" You're allowed to ask for the topic. That's not needy. That's just turning the lights on so you can stop imagining monsters in the dark.

Small change. Massive difference. For both of you.
