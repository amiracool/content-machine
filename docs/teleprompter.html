POV: Interrupting Someone With ADHD Mid-Thought 🧠

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

POV: you interrupt someone with ADHD mid-thought.

Congrats. That idea? It's gone. Forever.

[B-ROLL]

Not "give me a second and I'll remember." Not "it'll come back to me." I mean *gone.* Deleted. Formatted. Like it was never there.

And here's the part nobody talks about — we can *feel* it leaving. There's this half-second where we're reaching for it, and it's like watching a balloon float up past the ceiling. You can see it. You just can't grab it.

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever said "wait, what was I saying?" and felt genuine grief about it — stay right here. This one's going to make you feel *seen.*

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Okay, real talk. If you have ADHD, you know this scenario way too well.

You finally caught a thought. A good one. Maybe the best one you've had all day. It was fully formed, it was going somewhere, and you were *excited* to say it.

[B-ROLL]

Then someone jumps in. "Oh, that reminds me—"

And just like that, your brain does a full system wipe.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the problem. Most people don't understand that for us, a thought isn't stored — it's *held.* Like carrying water in your hands. The second something bumps you, it spills. And you cannot un-spill water.

So today I'm going to break down *why* this actually happens in the ADHD brain, why it feels so weirdly emotional, and — the part you actually came for — how to hold onto your thoughts without white-knuckling every conversation.

By the end of this, you'll finally have language for a thing you've been apologizing for your whole life. Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: It's not forgetting. It's working memory dropping the ball.

Here's the thing everyone gets wrong. When your thought vanishes, you didn't *forget* it. You never got to *save* it in the first place.

[B-ROLL]

Working memory is like your brain's sticky notes. It's the temporary space where you hold information while you're using it. In the ADHD brain, that space is smaller and way more fragile. So while you're actively holding a thought, you're using nearly all of it just to *keep* the thought alive.

Then an interruption comes in. And your brain has to make a choice: process the new thing, or keep holding the old thing. Guess which one wins? The loud, immediate, new thing. Every time.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like having one browser tab open. Just one. Someone sends you a new link, it opens in the same tab, and your original page is just... gone. No back button. No history.

So the next time someone with ADHD says "I lost my thought," understand — it's not carelessness. The hardware genuinely can't hold two things at once.

Which brings me to *why* losing it feels so absurdly dramatic.

Section 2: The grief is real — and it's about more than one thought.

Let's talk about why losing a thought feels like losing a tiny piece of yourself.

[B-ROLL]

When you have ADHD, good thoughts don't come on a schedule. They come in *bursts.* You might go hours feeling foggy and scattered, and then — boom — one perfect, clear idea. So when that idea shows up, it's not just an idea. It's proof. Proof that your brain still works. Proof you had something worth saying.

[B-ROLL]

So when it disappears, it doesn't feel like "oops, I'll think of another one." It feels like you *had* the good version of yourself for a second, and now it's gone, and you don't know when it's coming back.

And the worst part? People see your reaction and think you're being dramatic over nothing. "It's just a thought, relax." No. For us, thoughts aren't a renewable resource in that moment. They're rare.

[B-ROLL]

Naming this matters. Because if you understand that the grief is real, you stop shaming yourself for feeling it. And you can actually explain it to the people around you.

Speaking of which — let's talk about how to protect the thought before it escapes.

Section 3: Learn the "hold" signal — and teach it to your people.

Here's your first real tool. It's stupidly simple and it changes everything.

When you're mid-thought and someone starts talking, you need a physical signal that means "give me two seconds, I'm holding something fragile."

[B-ROLL]

Could be a raised finger. Could be a flat palm. Could be you literally saying "wait — let me finish this before it flies away." The point is, it buys you the half-second you need to land the thought instead of losing it in the crash.

But here's the game-changer: teach the signal to the people you talk to most. Your partner. Your best friend. Your coworker.

[B-ROLL]

Because most interruptions aren't rude. People just don't know that jumping in costs you the entire thought. Once your people *know* — once they understand that their "oh that reminds me!" is a delete button — they'll actually wait. And that small pause is the difference between finishing your idea and mourning it.

It reframes it too. You're not being difficult. You're just running different software, and you've given them the manual.

Now let's make sure the thought survives even if nobody's around to help.

Section 4: Externalize it before your brain can drop it.

Your working memory is unreliable. So stop trusting it. Get the thought *out of your head* the second you have it.

[B-ROLL]

This is the golden rule of ADHD: if it's in your head, it's at risk. If it's out of your head, it's safe.

Voice memos are your best friend. Talking to text. A notes app pinned to your home screen. A little notebook in your pocket. Even a text to yourself. Doesn't matter which — matters that you *capture* it in the first three seconds, before the balloon floats off.

[B-ROLL]

And don't wait for the "perfect" way to say it. Capture it messy. "Ugh — the plant thing — Tuesday — call mom." That's enough. Your brain can decode a messy note later. What it can't do is retrieve a thought it never wrote down.

Here's the mindset shift. You are not lazy for needing to write everything down. You are *smart* for building a system around how your brain actually works instead of how you *wish* it worked.

One more — and this one's for the people around us.

Section 5: For the interrupters — here's your cheat code.

If you *don't* have ADHD, but you love someone who does, this section is your gift to them.

[B-ROLL]

When you notice they're mid-thought — don't jump in. Even if you have the *perfect* thing to add. Even if you're worried you'll forget *your* point. Write yours down. Let them finish theirs.

Because here's the math: your thought will probably come back. Theirs won't.

[B-ROLL]

And if you accidentally interrupt — because it happens, we're all human — don't just say "sorry, go ahead." That doesn't help. Instead, ask: "wait, what were you about to say? I want to hear it." That little prompt sometimes catches the thought right before it fully disappears.

[B-ROLL]

That one sentence tells us two things. One: you noticed. Two: what we were saying actually mattered to you. And honestly? That feels better than the thought itself.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So here's the truth. Losing a thought mid-sentence isn't a character flaw. It's working memory doing exactly what ADHD working memory does. And now you've got the words *and* the tools to work with it instead of against it.

[B-ROLL]

If this made you feel understood — even a little — hit subscribe. This whole channel exists to hand you the language for stuff you've spent your whole life apologizing for. You're not too much. You're just running different software.

[B-ROLL]

And next, go watch my video "POV: trying to fall asleep with anxiety" — because if losing thoughts in the *day* is bad, wait till your brain replays that thing you said in 2014 at 2 a.m.

I'll see you there. Now go write down whatever you just thought of. Seriously. Right now. Before it's gone.
