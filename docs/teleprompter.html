My Honest Response to the "ADHD Is Overdiagnosed" Argument Everyone Keeps Sharing

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

There's an article going around right now. Maybe you've seen it. The headline says ADHD is "overdiagnosed." That we're handing out labels like candy. That half these people just need to put their phone down and focus.

And every time it gets shared, my inbox fills up with the same message: *"See? I told you it wasn't real. I told you you were just lazy."*

So I'm not going to rant. I'm going to do something better.

I'm going to actually answer the argument. Fairly. Honestly. Including the part they get right — because they do get one part right, and nobody's talking about it.

[B-ROLL]

Stay with me. By the end, you'll never lose this argument again.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you're probably in.

You finally got diagnosed. Or you're waiting to be. And it felt like the first time your whole life made sense. The forgotten appointments. The half-finished projects. The exhaustion of pretending you had it together.

And then this argument shows up. On the news. From your uncle at dinner. From a comment section you should've never opened.

"It's a trend." "Everyone thinks they have it now." "You're just looking for an excuse."

And here's the cruel part — it makes you doubt yourself. The one thing that finally explained your life, and now you're wondering if you made it up.

[B-ROLL]

So today I'm giving you three things. What the overdiagnosis argument gets wrong. The one uncomfortable thing it gets right. And exactly what to say when someone throws this at you.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: "More diagnoses" doesn't mean "overdiagnosis"

Here's the trick built into the whole argument. People see diagnosis numbers going up, and they assume that means we're diagnosing people who don't have it.

But those aren't the same thing.

Think about left-handedness. For decades, the rates were low. Kids were forced to write with their right hand. Then we stopped punishing it — and suddenly the numbers "exploded." Did left-handedness become a trend? No. We just stopped hiding it.

[B-ROLL]

That's what's happening with ADHD. We got better at spotting it in girls, who were missed for decades because they don't bounce off the walls — they daydream and mask. We got better at spotting it in adults who spent thirty years thinking they were just broken.

Rising numbers can mean we're finally catching people we used to fail. That's not a scandal. That's progress.

So when someone says "the numbers are up, therefore it's fake," ask them one question: *Compared to what? A time when we barely diagnosed anyone correctly?*

[B-ROLL]

Now — let's talk about the part they actually get right. Because ignoring it is how you lose the argument.

Section 2: The thing the argument gets right (and why saying so makes you stronger)

Deep breath. Here it is.

Some people are being misdiagnosed.

Not the way the critics think. But it happens. A ten-minute appointment with an overworked doctor. A tired parent, a stressed teacher, a checklist filled out too fast. Anxiety that looks like ADHD. Trauma that looks like ADHD. Burnout that looks like ADHD. Sleep deprivation that looks *exactly* like ADHD.

[B-ROLL]

That's real. A rushed system produces sloppy diagnoses. But look closely at what that actually proves.

It doesn't prove ADHD is fake. It proves our system is under-resourced. The problem isn't too many diagnoses — it's low-quality assessments. Those are opposite problems with opposite solutions.

Here's why admitting this matters. When you say, "Yeah, bad assessments are a real issue, and that's exactly why we need better ones" — you take the wind out of their sails. You're not defensive. You're not fragile. You sound like the reasonable one in the room. Because you are.

[B-ROLL]

The strongest response to a bad argument isn't to deny everything. It's to concede what's true and expose what isn't.

Which brings me to the biggest hole in this whole thing.

Section 3: Nobody's asking about the people going undiagnosed

The overdiagnosis crowd has a spotlight. And they point it in exactly one direction — the people who *might not* need help.

But turn that spotlight around. Look at the other side of the room.

[B-ROLL]

The adults who spent forty years thinking they were failures. The kids in underfunded schools who never got assessed at all. The women, the people of color, the poor — the groups research shows are *consistently* under-diagnosed and under-treated.

For every story about someone who got a label they didn't need, there are people out there right now white-knuckling through life with zero support. Drowning quietly. Blaming themselves.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the imbalance nobody names. An unnecessary diagnosis is a fixable mistake — you reassess, you correct it. But a *missed* diagnosis? That's years of someone's life. That's the marriage that ended. The career that stalled. The kid who grew up believing they were stupid.

So when someone worries we're diagnosing too many people, I ask: *Why aren't you equally worried about the ones we're missing?*

Because usually? They've never even considered them.

[B-ROLL]

Section 4: The word "excuse" is doing all the heavy lifting

Let's talk about the real message hiding under this argument. Strip away the studies and the statistics, and what's usually left is one word: *excuse.*

"You're just using it as an excuse."

[B-ROLL]

But watch what an actual diagnosis does. It doesn't say, "Now you get to stop trying." It says, "Now you finally understand *why* trying the normal way never worked — so you can try in a way that actually fits your brain."

An explanation is not an excuse. Glasses aren't an excuse for bad eyesight. A wheelchair ramp isn't an excuse for not walking. They're tools that let people participate.

[B-ROLL]

I've watched people go from hating themselves to building systems that work. From "I'm lazy" to "I need body-doubling, external deadlines, and reminders that actually reach me." That's not someone hiding. That's someone finally showing up.

The people calling it an excuse have it exactly backwards. For most of us, the diagnosis is where the *real* effort begins.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: What to actually say when this comes up

Okay. Practical part. Here's your response when someone hits you with this. Three sentences. Memorize them.

One: *"Diagnoses going up doesn't mean overdiagnosis — it often means we're finally catching people we used to miss."*

Two: *"If you're worried about bad assessments, we agree — the fix is better assessments, not fewer people getting help."*

Three: *"And I'm way more worried about everyone going undiagnosed than the rare person who got it wrong."*

[B-ROLL]

That's it. Calm. Fair. Impossible to twist.

And if the conversation is happening inside your own head — if *you're* the one doubting whether it's real — I want you to hear this.

[B-ROLL]

The fact that you struggle to believe your own diagnosis? That's not proof it's fake. That's often the diagnosis itself talking. The years of being told you weren't trying hard enough don't disappear overnight. That voice lingers.

But an article shared by someone who's never met you doesn't get to overrule the doctor who assessed you and the lived experience of your entire life.

You didn't invent your struggles to win sympathy. You survived them. There's a difference. And you know it.

[B-ROLL]
