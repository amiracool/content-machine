# They Keep Telling Women Their ADHD Is "Just Anxiety" — Here's the Dangerous Truth Behind That Mistake

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

They told her it was anxiety.

For twelve years.

Twelve years of medication that didn't work. Twelve years of "just calm down." Twelve years of thinking she was broken.

Then one doctor asked one question — and everything changed.

If you're a woman who's been told your struggles are "just anxiety," "just stress," or "just hormones"... I need you to hear this. Because there's a good chance the people meant to help you missed something huge.

[B-ROLL]

Let me explain why so many women's ADHD gets buried under an anxiety label — and what it's actually costing them.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you might know all too well.

You feel scattered. You can't finish things. Your brain won't shut up at night. You're exhausted from trying to keep it all together — and everyone just keeps telling you you're anxious.

So you get the anxiety diagnosis. Maybe you get the pills. And some of it helps... a little. But deep down, something still doesn't fit.

That's because for a huge number of women, the anxiety isn't the root problem.

It's a symptom.

[B-ROLL]

I've spent years talking to people about neurodiversity, and this is one of the most common stories I hear from women. And by the end of this video, you're going to understand exactly why this mix-up happens, how to spot the difference, and what to actually do about it.

Because this isn't a small mistake. It's a mistake that steals years of people's lives.

BODY

Section 1: The anxiety is real — but it's often a reaction, not the cause

Here's the first thing nobody explains properly.

When a woman with undiagnosed ADHD walks into a doctor's office, she usually *is* anxious. Genuinely, painfully anxious. So the doctor isn't lying — they're just seeing the smoke and missing the fire.

Think about it like a smoke alarm going off in your house. The alarm is real. The noise is real. But if you only treat the alarm — you keep silencing it — you never notice the wiring behind the wall that's actually overheating.

[B-ROLL]

ADHD is the wiring. The anxiety is the alarm.

When you spend every single day forgetting appointments, missing deadlines, and feeling like you're one mistake away from everyone finding out you can't cope — of course you become anxious. That's not a disorder on its own. That's a completely logical response to living with an untreated brain difference.

So treating only the anxiety is like handing someone earplugs while the house burns down.

And that leads us straight into why women specifically get caught in this trap.

Section 2: Women were left out of the research — so we're using the wrong picture

For decades, ADHD was studied almost entirely in boys. Hyperactive, disruptive, bouncing-off-the-walls boys.

So that became the picture in everyone's head. The kid who can't sit still. The boy causing chaos in class.

But a lot of women don't look like that at all.

[B-ROLL]

Girls with ADHD often turn it *inward*. The hyperactivity becomes racing thoughts instead of a racing body. The impulsivity becomes emotional overwhelm. Instead of disrupting the class, she's the one staring out the window, lost in her own head, quietly falling behind while looking perfectly fine on the outside.

She learns to mask. She becomes the "sensitive one," the "dreamer," the "worrier."

And here's the cruel part — masking takes enormous energy. Holding it together all day, performing "normal," is exhausting. And what does that exhaustion and hyper-alertness look like to a doctor?

It looks exactly like anxiety.

So she gets diagnosed with the thing that's easiest to see, using research that was never built for her in the first place.

[B-ROLL]

Which brings us to the moment where this actually starts to hurt people.

Section 3: The wrong treatment makes it worse — not better

This is the part that makes me genuinely angry.

When you treat ADHD as if it's anxiety, the standard advice can actively backfire.

A common approach for anxiety is to slow down, avoid stimulation, and reduce your load. Reasonable, right? But an ADHD brain is often *under*-stimulated. It's not craving less — it's desperate for the right kind of engagement and structure.

[B-ROLL]

It's like a car that won't start because it's out of fuel — and someone keeps telling you to drive slower. You're not going too fast. You've got nothing in the tank.

Some women also get put on medication that dulls everything down, when what their brain actually needed was help with focus, motivation, and follow-through. So they feel foggier, flatter, and even *more* like a failure — because now the "solution" isn't working either.

And every time a treatment fails, the story in her head gets louder: *It must be me. I'm the problem. I'm just weak.*

[B-ROLL]

That's the real damage. It's not just years wasted. It's a woman slowly being convinced she's broken, when she was never broken at all. She was misread.

Let's talk about how to actually tell the difference.

Section 4: How to spot the difference for yourself

I'm not a doctor, and this isn't a diagnosis. But there are some honest questions worth sitting with.

Ask yourself: does my anxiety have an obvious source? Classic anxiety usually attaches to specific fears or situations. ADHD-driven anxiety often feels more like a constant background hum — a low-level dread that's there even when nothing is technically wrong.

[B-ROLL]

Ask: was I like this as a kid? ADHD doesn't suddenly appear at thirty. If you were the daydreamer, the disorganised one, the "so much potential if she'd just apply herself" kid — that matters.

Ask: does the anxiety spike specifically around focus, deadlines, and forgetting things? If your worst moments are tied to executive function — starting tasks, finishing them, remembering them — that's a clue this might be bigger than anxiety alone.

And ask: has anxiety treatment alone ever fully worked? If you've done the therapy, tried the meds, and something still feels fundamentally unaddressed... trust that instinct. That gap you feel is real data.

[B-ROLL]

Write your answers down. Bring them to a professional. Because self-awareness is powerful, but the next step needs the right person in the room.

Which is exactly what I want to leave you with.

Section 5: Getting reassessed is not being "difficult" — it's being right

A lot of women hesitate to push back. They don't want to seem like they're chasing a label or doubting the experts.

But here's the reframe I want to give you.

Asking to be properly assessed for ADHD isn't being dramatic. It's not attention-seeking. It's advocating for your own brain — which nobody else is going to do for you.

[B-ROLL]

If a mechanic told you your car was fine but it kept breaking down, you'd get a second opinion. You wouldn't just accept years of a car that doesn't run. Your mind deserves at least that much respect.

Find a professional who understands how ADHD presents in women. Say the words out loud: "I've been treated for anxiety, but I think something's being missed." A good clinician will take that seriously.

And if they don't — find another one. You are allowed to keep looking until someone actually sees you.

[B-ROLL]

Remember that woman from the start of this video? Twelve years of the wrong label? The question that changed everything was simply: "Has anyone ever asked about your attention and focus — not just your worry?"

One question. And suddenly her whole life made sense in a new way. Not because she was finally broken enough to name — but because someone finally looked at the wiring, not just the alarm.
