# My Response to the "Everyone Has ADHD Now" Claim That's Flooding Your Feed

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"Everyone has ADHD now."

You've seen it. In the comments. In the group chat. Maybe your own family said it at dinner last week.

And if you actually *have* ADHD, that sentence doesn't just annoy you. It stings. Because buried inside that eye-roll is a quiet accusation: that your struggle isn't real. That you're just trendy. That you jumped on a bandwagon.

[B-ROLL]

So today, I'm giving my honest response. Not defensive. Not angry. Just the truth that this take gets completely wrong — and the part it accidentally gets right.

Stay with me. Because by the end, you'll know exactly what to say the next time someone hits you with it.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you're actually in.

You spent years thinking something was wrong with you. Maybe you got diagnosed recently. Maybe you're still figuring it out. And for the first time, things finally *made sense*.

[B-ROLL]

Then the internet decided ADHD was a personality trend. And now the thing that gave you answers is being treated like a costume people put on for clout.

So you're stuck. You don't want to be one of the "everyone has ADHD" people. But you also know your brain is not neurotypical. That's an impossible spot to sit in.

Here's my promise. In the next few minutes, I'm going to break down where this claim comes from, why it *feels* true even though it's misleading, and how to hold onto your own truth without needing to convince anyone.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: The claim isn't about you — it's about visibility

The first thing to understand: "everyone has ADHD now" is not a medical observation. It's a reaction to *visibility*.

For decades, ADHD was mostly diagnosed in hyperactive little boys bouncing off classroom walls. Everyone else — the daydreamers, the chronically overwhelmed, the women masking it all — slipped straight through the cracks.

[B-ROLL]

Then the internet handed people language. Suddenly someone describes forgetting to eat because they were locked into a task for six hours, and thousands of people go, "wait, that's a *symptom*?"

Think of it like this. Imagine glasses were rare and expensive for a hundred years. Most people just squinted through blurry lives, assuming that was normal. Then glasses got cheap and available. Suddenly you'd see people wearing them everywhere.

[B-ROLL]

Would you say "everyone has bad eyesight now"? No. The eyesight was always there. You just couldn't *see* it before.

That's what's happening. It's not an explosion of ADHD. It's an explosion of recognition.

And that leads to the part people get confused about.

Section 2: More diagnoses doesn't mean fake diagnoses

Here's where the logic breaks.

People see rising diagnosis numbers and assume that means the diagnosis has gotten *loose*. Like doctors are handing it out like candy.

[B-ROLL]

But rising numbers usually mean the *old* numbers were wrong. Specifically, too low.

Think about left-handedness. A hundred years ago, kids got their left hands smacked and tied behind their backs until they wrote with their right. Recorded left-handedness was tiny. Then we stopped forcing it — and the numbers "spiked."

[B-ROLL]

Did left-handedness suddenly become trendy? Of course not. We just stopped suppressing it and started counting it honestly.

Same thing here. When you finally include women, adults, and people who don't fit the hyperactive stereotype — of course the numbers rise. That's not a flood of fakers. That's a backlog of people who were missed.

[B-ROLL]

Now, does that mean *nobody* over-identifies online? No. And I'm not going to pretend it does. Which brings me to the uncomfortable part.

Section 3: The claim gets ONE thing right — and we should say so

I'm not here to tell you the "everyone has ADHD" crowd is a hundred percent wrong. Because there's a grain of truth in there, and pretending otherwise makes us look defensive.

Here's the honest bit.

A lot of what gets called "ADHD" online is just... being a human in a broken environment.

[B-ROLL]

Everyone struggles to focus when they're sleeping four hours a night. Everyone gets distracted with a phone engineered by billion-dollar companies to hijack attention. Everyone feels scattered when they're anxious, burnt out, or under-stimulated at a job they hate.

Those are real problems. But they're not necessarily ADHD.

The difference isn't "do you get distracted sometimes." It's "has this pattern shown up across your *entire life*, in *multiple settings*, causing *real impairment* — regardless of how good your sleep or diet or environment is?"

[B-ROLL]

That distinction matters. And here's the twist most people miss: defending it *helps* people with ADHD. Because when the label gets blurry, the people who genuinely need support get lost in the noise again.

So no, not everyone has ADHD. But it's completely fair to say a lot of people are struggling with attention. Those two things can both be true.

Section 4: Why "everyone has it" is such a comforting lie

Let's talk about why this phrase is so sticky. Because understanding this changes how you respond to it forever.

"Everyone has ADHD now" is emotionally comforting to the person saying it.

[B-ROLL]

Think about what it lets them avoid. If ADHD is real and common, then maybe *they* have some of those struggles too. Maybe the thing they've been calling laziness in themselves — or worse, calling laziness in you for years — was never a character flaw.

That's a scary door to open.

So "everyone has it now" is a way of slamming that door shut. It reframes a real condition as a fad, so nobody has to sit with the discomfort of "maybe I misjudged people. Maybe I misjudged myself."

[B-ROLL]

It's the same energy as "back in my day we just got on with it." That's not a fact. That's grief in a disguise. It's someone who *did* just get on with it — while suffering silently — and can't bear the idea that it didn't have to be that hard.

[B-ROLL]

Once you see that, the sting fades. Because you realize the phrase was never really about you. It's about *them* not wanting to look at something.

Section 5: What to actually say back

Okay. The practical part. Next time someone says "everyone has ADHD now," you don't need to win a debate. You need one calm sentence.

Here are three, depending on the vibe.

For the dismissive relative: "More people getting diagnosed doesn't mean it's fake — it means we used to miss most of it."

[B-ROLL]

For the skeptical friend who's actually curious: "There's a real difference between being distracted sometimes and a lifelong pattern that affects everything. I'm the second one."

For yourself, in your own head, when it stings: "Their doubt doesn't change my diagnosis. I don't need them to believe me to keep getting support."

[B-ROLL]

You don't owe anyone a TED talk. You don't have to prove your brain to people committed to misunderstanding it. Your job is to keep building the life and the systems that actually work for *you*.

Let the internet argue. You've got a diagnosis, a reason, and a path forward. That's worth more than winning a comment section.
