# POV: You finally start the task you've dreaded for 3 weeks — and it takes 4 minutes 🙃

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

POV: You finally start the task you've dreaded for three weeks.

And it takes... four minutes.

[B-ROLL]

Four minutes.

You spent 21 days avoiding something that took less time than microwaving a burrito.

And now you're sitting there like... *what was all that about?*

[B-ROLL]

If you just felt personally attacked, stay right here. Because I'm about to explain exactly why your brain does this — and how to make it stop.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the task you've been avoiding. Pick yours.

Replying to one email. Booking one appointment. Sending one text. Filling out one form.

[B-ROLL]

Something small. Something simple. Something that logically should take you five minutes.

But it's been sitting on your to-do list for weeks. Maybe months.

And every single day, you see it. You think about it. You feel that little pang of dread.

[B-ROLL]

And you move on.

Then one random day — for no reason you can explain — you just... do it. And it's done. And it was *nothing.*

Here's what I want you to understand: this is not laziness. This is not a character flaw. This is a nervous system thing. And if you have ADHD, anxiety, or you're just wired a little differently, this is textbook.

By the end of this video, you're gonna understand why your brain turns four-minute tasks into three-week mountains — and you'll have a real strategy to shrink those mountains back down.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: The task isn't the problem — the *feeling* about the task is

Here's the twist nobody tells you.

You're not avoiding the task. You're avoiding how the task makes you *feel.*

[B-ROLL]

Think about it. Replying to that email is physically easy. Your fingers work. You know what to type.

But somewhere in your brain, that email is tangled up with a feeling. Maybe it's shame — "I should've replied two weeks ago." Maybe it's uncertainty — "What if I say the wrong thing?" Maybe it's boredom so intense it feels like your brain is going to shut down.

The task is four minutes. The *feeling* is what's been following you around for 21 days.

[B-ROLL]

It's like having a splinter you refuse to look at. The splinter is tiny. But the dread of touching it feels enormous. So you leave it. And it just... throbs there. Reminding you.

So the first thing to understand is: when you feel that resistance, your brain isn't being dramatic for no reason. It's reacting to an emotion that got glued to a to-do list item.

Which means the fix isn't "try harder." It's "feel first." And I'll show you how in a second.

Section 2: Your brain scores tasks by emotion, not logic

Let me explain what's actually happening in your head.

Most people assume we prioritize tasks by importance. Deadline. Consequences. Logic.

ADHD and anxious brains don't work like that.

[B-ROLL]

Your brain runs on four things: novelty, interest, urgency, and challenge. If a task doesn't hit one of those, your brain literally struggles to generate the motivation to start it.

It's not that you *won't.* It's that the "go" signal isn't firing.

So a boring four-minute task? Your brain rates it as basically impossible. But rearranging your entire closet at 11pm? Suddenly you have the energy of a small country.

[B-ROLL]

This is why "just do it" advice feels insulting. The people giving it have a brain that produces motivation on demand. Yours quotes you a price. And the price for boring tasks is way too high.

Here's the reframe that changes everything: stop waiting to *feel* motivated. You're waiting for a bus that isn't coming. Motivation, for brains like ours, usually shows up *after* you start — not before.

[B-ROLL]

Which leads us perfectly to the actual strategy.

Section 3: Shrink the task until it's embarrassingly small

Here's the move. When a task feels like a mountain, you don't climb it. You make it smaller until it's just a bump.

Your brain resists "reply to that email." So don't do that.

Instead: "Open the email." That's it. That's the whole task.

[B-ROLL]

Not reply. Not write. Just open. You can close it right after if you want.

But here's the magic — nine times out of ten, once it's open, you just... keep going. Because remember Section 2? Starting is the hard part. Your brain was quoting you a huge price to *begin.* Once you've begun, the price drops.

[B-ROLL]

This is called the "ridiculously small first step." And it needs to feel almost stupid. If your first step still gives you anxiety, it's too big. Shrink it again.

"Clean the kitchen" becomes "pick up one cup." "Do my taxes" becomes "find the folder." "Call the doctor" becomes "look up the phone number."

[B-ROLL]

You are not lowering your standards. You're hacking the "start" barrier that your specific brain struggles with. Big difference.

Section 4: Externalize the dread so it stops haunting you

Now let's deal with the three weeks of suffering *before* the four minutes.

Because honestly? The task itself isn't the hard part. The hard part is carrying it around in your head, day after day, feeling bad about it.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the thing about ADHD and anxious brains: an unfinished task doesn't just sit quietly. It runs in the background, draining your energy, popping up at the worst times, usually at 3am.

So you need to get it out of your head and into the real world.

Write it down. On paper. On a sticky note stuck to your laptop. In a note on your phone that you actually see.

[B-ROLL]

But here's the key — don't write the giant scary version. Write the shrunk-down version from Section 3. "Open the email." Not "finally deal with this whole thing you've been avoiding and feel terrible about."

Your brain reads tone. If your to-do list sounds like it's disappointed in you, you'll avoid looking at it.

[B-ROLL]

When the dread lives on paper instead of in your head, two things happen. One, you stop using mental energy to remember it. And two, it loses its power. A sticky note that says "open the email" is a lot less scary than a vague cloud of guilt following you around for three weeks.

Section 5: Celebrate the four minutes — don't punish the three weeks

This is the step everyone skips, and it's the one that actually rewires things over time.

When you finally finish the four-minute task, what's your instinct?

[B-ROLL]

Probably to beat yourself up. "Why did I wait three weeks for THAT? I'm so pathetic. That was so easy, what is wrong with me?"

Stop. Right there.

Because here's what that does: it teaches your brain that finishing tasks leads to *punishment.* And your brain is paying attention. Next time there's a task, it remembers — "last time we did this, we felt like garbage afterward." So it avoids even harder.

[B-ROLL]

Instead, when you finish, you celebrate. Genuinely. Out loud if you have to. "I did the thing. It's done. That's a win."

[B-ROLL]

I know it feels silly. Do it anyway. You're not celebrating because it was hard. You're celebrating because you *started something your brain resisted* — and that's the actual skill you're building.

Over time, your brain starts to associate finishing with feeling good. And that makes the *next* task easier to start. That's the whole game. That's how you break the three-week-dread cycle for real.

[B-ROLL]

The dread will never fully disappear — you've got a brain that's wired for it. But you can absolutely make it smaller, quieter, and shorter. Four minutes of work, minus three weeks of suffering. That's the goal.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So here's what I want you to do right now. Pause this video, pick the one task you've been avoiding, and shrink it down to something ridiculously small. Open the email. Find the phone number. Pick up one cup. That's it.

[B-ROLL]

And if this made you feel seen — if you've been carrying around a to-do list that sounds disappointed in you — subscribe. This whole channel exists to explain your brain to you, without shame and without the "just try harder" nonsense.

[B-ROLL]

Next, go watch my video on why your brain picks midnight to suddenly become productive — because trust me, that chaos has an explanation too, and understanding it is a game-changer.

[B-ROLL]

Now go do your four-minute task. I believe in you. See you in the next one.
