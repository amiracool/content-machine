# When ADHD People Try to "Just Do One Small Task" 😅

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When ADHD people try to "just do one small task" and reorganize the entire kitchen instead. 😅

Watch this.

[B-ROLL]

You came in to rinse ONE cup.

That was the plan. Rinse the cup. Walk away. Live your life.

[B-ROLL]

But now the spice rack is alphabetized, the fridge is empty, and the cup? Still. In. The. Sink.

If you've ever "quickly done one thing" and lost an entire afternoon, this is for you. Because this isn't laziness. And I'm going to show you exactly what's actually happening in your brain.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you know too well.

You decide to do one tiny task. Rinse the cup. Reply to one email. Grab your charger.

And somehow, forty-five minutes later, you're doing seven things you never planned to do. None of them are the original task.

[B-ROLL]

Then the guilt hits. "Why can't I just do the simple thing? Everyone else can do the simple thing."

I need you to hear this. It's not a discipline problem. It's a wiring difference. Your ADHD brain doesn't process tasks in a straight line. It processes them like a pinball machine.

By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly why this happens. And I'll give you three specific tricks to actually finish the one thing you started. Let's go.

---

BODY

Section 1: Your brain doesn't see "one task." It sees a spiderweb.

Here's the truth. A neurotypical brain sees "rinse the cup" as a closed loop. Start. Finish. Done.

Your brain sees "rinse the cup" and instantly opens twelve related tabs.

[B-ROLL]

You walk to the sink. You notice the sink is dirty. Which reminds you the sponge is gross. Which reminds you the sponges are under the sink. Which reminds you the cabinet under the sink is a disaster. And now you're on your knees pulling out cleaning supplies you forgot you owned.

Every task is connected to five other tasks. Your brain sees all of them at once. It's not scattered. It's actually seeing too many connections.

Think of it like this. Everyone else gets a to-do list. You got a to-do web. And the second you touch one strand, the whole thing vibrates.

[B-ROLL]

So the first step isn't fixing the behavior. It's understanding that your brain is doing exactly what it's designed to do. Now let's work WITH it instead of against it.

Section 2: The "just" is a trap. Kill the word "just."

Notice how it always starts with "just."

"I'll just rinse this cup." "I'll just answer one text." "I'll just tidy up real quick."

[B-ROLL]

That word "just" is lying to you. It tells your brain the task is small and infinite, so there's no urgency and no boundary. And a task with no boundary is a task that expands to fill your entire day.

Here's the fix. Give the task a physical finish line and a time limit. Out loud.

Instead of "I'll just rinse the cup," say: "I'm going to rinse this one cup, put it in the rack, and walk out of the kitchen. Two minutes."

[B-ROLL]

You're literally drawing a map for your brain. Start point. End point. Exit route. When you name the exit, your brain stops treating it like an open web and starts treating it like a closed loop.

Try it once today. Say the full sentence out loud, including how you leave the room. It feels silly. It works.

Now, what about when you're already halfway into the chaos?

Section 3: Use the "capture list" so your brain can let go.

Here's why you spiral. When you notice the messy cabinet mid-task, your brain screams, "Do it NOW or you'll forget forever."

And honestly? It's right. You probably will forget. So your brain won't let you leave.

[B-ROLL]

The solution isn't to ignore the new task. That fights your brain. The solution is to give your brain proof that the task is safe.

Keep a notepad or a notes app open. The second you notice "the cabinet is a mess," you don't do it. You write it down. "Clean under-sink cabinet." Then you go back to the cup.

[B-ROLL]

That tiny act tells your brain, "It's captured. We won't lose it. You can let go now." And most of the time, that pressure just... releases.

Think of it like a bouncer at a club. New tasks keep showing up at the door demanding to come in RIGHT NOW. The capture list is the bouncer saying, "Cool, you're on the list. Wait outside." Order restored.

[B-ROLL]

Do this for a week and watch how many spirals you avoid. You're not suppressing the thoughts. You're parking them.

Section 4: Body-double the boring stuff.

Sometimes the reason you never finish the one task is that the one task is boring. And your ADHD brain physically cannot generate motivation for boring.

That's not weakness. That's a dopamine difference. Boring tasks give your brain almost nothing to work with.

[B-ROLL]

So you need borrowed momentum. This is called body doubling. You either do the task with another person present, on a video call, or with a "study with me" video playing.

The presence of another human, even a virtual one, gives your brain just enough accountability and stimulation to actually move.

[B-ROLL]

Can't get a person? Narrate it out loud to yourself like a chaotic sports announcer. "And she picks up the cup. The crowd goes wild." I'm not joking. Turning the boring thing into a bit tricks your brain into engagement.

The point is, stop expecting a boring task to feel motivating on its own. It won't. Build the motivation in from the outside.

Section 5: Forgive the spiral. Seriously.

Here's the part nobody talks about. The reason ADHD "one task" chaos ruins your whole day usually isn't the chaos.

It's the shame that comes after.

[B-ROLL]

You lose an hour, then you spend the next three hours calling yourself lazy, broken, and behind. And THAT is what actually wrecks your productivity, not the detour.

So try reframing it. When you catch yourself mid-spiral, don't panic. Just say, "Oh, classic ADHD web. Let me re-anchor." Then find your original task and finish that one thing.

[B-ROLL]

One finished task rebuilds your confidence way faster than beating yourself up ever will. And honestly, look, sometimes the alphabetized spice rack is a genuinely nice bonus. You did a thing. It counts.

Your brain isn't broken. It's just running different software. Once you stop fighting the software and start writing better instructions for it, everything gets easier.
