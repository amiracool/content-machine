# POV: Setting 7 Alarms and Still Being Late Because Time Blindness Is Real ⏰

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

It's 6:00 AM. Your first alarm goes off.

You're confident. You've got SEVEN alarms today. There is no possible way you're late.

[B-ROLL]

6:15. Snooze.

6:30. "I'll get up on the next one."

6:45. You turn one OFF because it's "annoying."

[B-ROLL]

And then somehow… it's 7:52. You have to leave at 7:55. And you're still in bed, staring at the ceiling, asking the universe: *how.*

If this is your life, stay with me. Because it's not laziness. It's not a willpower problem. It's time blindness. And by the end of this video, you're actually going to understand your own brain.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

Here's the thing nobody told you.

You're not "bad with time." Your brain genuinely does not feel time passing the way other people's brains do. For a lot of ADHD and neurodivergent folks, time isn't this steady river flowing in the background. It's more like… there's "now" and there's "not now." That's it. Two time zones.

[B-ROLL]

So when a task is in the "not now" box, it may as well not exist. And then it SLAMS into the "now" box with zero warning, and suddenly you're sprinting.

I've lived this my whole life. The guilt, the "why can't I just be normal," the people who think you don't care.

So here's what I'm going to give you. First, why your seven alarms actually make the problem *worse.* Then, three strategies that work *with* a time-blind brain instead of fighting it. These are the things that genuinely changed my relationship with being on time. Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Why Seven Alarms Backfire

Here's the brutal truth: the more alarms you set, the less any single alarm means.

[B-ROLL]

Think about a car alarm in a parking lot. The first time you hear one, you look up. The hundredth time? You don't even notice it anymore. Your brain filters it out as background noise. It's called habituation.

That's exactly what happens with your alarm stack. By alarm number four, your brain has learned that an alarm doesn't mean "get up." It means "there will be another one soon." You've literally trained yourself to ignore the thing that's supposed to save you.

[B-ROLL]

So the fix isn't *more* alarms. It's making ONE alarm actually mean something. We'll get to how in a second.

But first, let's deal with the invisible part of the problem.

Section 2: Make Time Visible

You can't manage something you can't see. And for a time-blind brain, time is completely invisible.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the shift. Stop *reading* time and start *seeing* it. There's a huge difference between a clock that says "7:15" and a visual timer that shows you a shrinking red wedge disappearing in real time.

When I say "you have 20 minutes," your brain hears "a lot." But when you watch that red section physically shrink? Your body finally gets it. Suddenly 20 minutes feels like 20 minutes.

[B-ROLL]

Try it. Get a visual timer — there are physical ones and free apps. Put it where you can actually see it while you get ready. Not on your phone, where it hides behind nine other apps. Out in the open.

This one trick does something magical: it turns "not now" into "now, and I can watch it leaving." And once you can see time, you can start to *estimate* it. Which brings me to the biggest mistake almost all of us make.

Section 3: Stop Planning "Point-to-Point" Time

Quick question. When you plan what time to leave, what do you think about?

Probably just the drive, right? "It's a 15-minute drive, so I'll leave 15 minutes before."

[B-ROLL]

And that's the trap. Because the drive is maybe 20% of what actually has to happen. You forgot about the other stuff — the invisible tasks that live in the gaps.

[B-ROLL]

Finding your keys. Realizing you have no clean shirt. Walking back inside for the thing you forgot. Those "two-second" tasks that are secretly four minutes each.

So here's the fix. Don't plan point-to-point. Plan *backwards* from the moment you need to be sitting in that meeting or that chair. And write down every single micro-step between now and then.

[B-ROLL]

Then — and this is key — add a buffer. Whatever time you think it'll take, add 50%. Not because you're slow. Because time blindness means your estimate is almost always *optimistic.* We chronically think things take less time than they do. Padding the plan isn't pessimism. It's accuracy.

Section 4: Anchor Your Alarm to an Action

Okay, back to that single meaningful alarm. Here's how you make it work.

An alarm that just says "wake up" is useless, because waking up isn't a task you can *do.* It's vague. Your brain can't grab onto it.

[B-ROLL]

So label your alarm with a *physical first action.* Not "get ready." Something like: "Feet on the floor and turn on the big light." One concrete move.

Because the hardest part is never the whole morning. It's the transition — breaking out of "not now" into "now." If you give your brain a single tiny physical action, you create momentum. And momentum is everything for us.

[B-ROLL]

Bonus move: put the alarm across the room. Not to torture you. To force the transition. Once you're standing, you've already won the hardest fight of the morning.

Section 5: Build in a "Launch Pad"

Last one, and it's the one that saves me most mornings.

[B-ROLL]

Create a launch pad. One single spot by the door where everything you need to leave lives. Keys. Wallet. Headphones. Charger. Water bottle. Whatever your "where is it" items are.

[B-ROLL]

The reason we're late is so often those last five minutes of chaos, tearing the house apart for one item. The launch pad deletes that entire category of disaster. You set it up the *night before,* when future-you isn't panicking, so morning-you just grabs and goes.

And here's the mindset shift underneath all of this: you're not trying to become a different person. You're building ramps for the brain you actually have. The goal was never "try harder." It's "need less willpower."
