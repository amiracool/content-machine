# The "Everyone Has a Little ADHD Now" Claim — My Honest Response

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"Everyone's a little bit ADHD these days."

If I had a pound for every time someone said that to me, I could fund my own research study.

And here's what nobody tells you: that one sentence — said casually, usually with a shrug — does more damage to actual ADHD people than almost anything else online right now.

[B-ROLL]

So today I'm responding to it. Properly. Not with anger. With facts, with lived experience, and with the part of this conversation everyone keeps skipping over.

Stick with me, because by the end you'll know exactly how to handle it next time someone says it to your face.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Let me guess your situation.

You either have ADHD, you think you might, or you love someone who does. And you've watched this phrase go absolutely viral. TikToks. Comment sections. That one relative at dinner.

[B-ROLL]

And it stings. Because when everyone "has a little ADHD," suddenly *your* ADHD looks like an excuse. A personality quirk. Something you should just push through, like everyone else supposedly does.

The problem isn't that people are curious about ADHD. That's good. The problem is that this phrase flattens a genuine neurodevelopmental condition into a relatable meme.

Here's my promise. In the next few minutes, I'm going to show you the exact difference between "a little bit ADHD" and actual ADHD. I'll give you language to explain it. And I'll hand you a response so clear that the next person who says it to you actually gets it.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Everyone has traits. Almost nobody has the disorder.

Here's the truth that gets buried: everyone experiences ADHD *traits* sometimes. But traits are not a disorder.

Everyone forgets their keys. Everyone gets distracted. Everyone procrastinates before a deadline. That's just being human.

[B-ROLL]

But ADHD isn't "I did a distracted thing once." ADHD is a persistent pattern that shows up across your *whole life*, in multiple settings, causing real impairment.

Think of it like this. Everyone feels sad sometimes. That doesn't mean everyone has clinical depression. Feeling sad after a bad day is a normal emotion. Depression is a condition that changes how you function for weeks, months, years.

[B-ROLL]

Same logic. A trait is a moment. A disorder is a pattern with consequences.

So when someone says "everyone's a little ADHD," what they actually mean is "everyone has traits." And they're right about that. They're just using the wrong word for it.

Which brings me to the part they always miss.

Section 2: It's not about *having* symptoms. It's about how much they cost you.

This is the piece the viral version completely ignores.

Diagnosis isn't a checklist you tick to feel special. To actually be diagnosed, those symptoms have to significantly interfere with your life. Work. Relationships. Finances. Your health.

[B-ROLL]

Yes, everyone procrastinates. But do they lose jobs over it? Do they miss rent because the paperwork felt physically impossible to start? Do they burn out cycling between hyperfocus and total shutdown?

Here's an analogy. Lots of people occasionally have a drink and feel a bit off the next day. That's not the same as an addiction. The difference isn't the substance. It's the *cost*. The impairment. The way it hijacks your life.

[B-ROLL]

ADHD is the same. The symptoms alone don't define it. The price you pay for them does.

So when someone says "oh I'm a bit like that too," a fair question is: does it cost you your job? Your relationships? Your sense of self-worth every single day?

Because for us, it does. And that gap — the gap between annoyance and impairment — is the entire conversation.

Section 3: The "relatable" version erases how hard we've worked to cope.

Here's something that genuinely upsets me.

When people say "everyone's a little ADHD," they don't see the invisible effort underneath our lives.

[B-ROLL]

The systems. The alarms. The lists to remind us of the lists. The exhausting mental energy it takes just to appear "normal." That's called masking, and it is genuinely draining.

Think of a duck on a pond. On the surface, calm and gliding. Underneath, the legs are paddling like mad. That's an ADHD brain getting through an ordinary Tuesday.

[B-ROLL]

So when we finally manage to look like we've "got it together," and someone shrugs and says "yeah everyone struggles with that," it erases every ounce of that hidden labour.

It's not that they're being cruel. Most people genuinely don't know. But that's exactly why we have to explain it — kindly, clearly, and without shrinking ourselves down to make them comfortable.

Section 4: Awareness went up. That's not the same as "everyone has it."

Let's tackle the sneaky assumption hiding inside the phrase.

More people are being diagnosed now. That's true. But that doesn't mean ADHD is "trendy" or that everyone suddenly has it.

[B-ROLL]

For decades, ADHD was massively underdiagnosed — especially in women, in adults, in people who didn't fit the hyperactive-little-boy stereotype.

Here's the comparison. Left-handedness "spiked" in the twentieth century. Did humans suddenly evolve? No. We just *stopped forcing* left-handed kids to write with their right hand. The trait was always there. We finally stopped punishing it and started counting it.

[B-ROLL]

ADHD is having its left-handed moment. We're not seeing an explosion of new cases. We're seeing people who were always there — finally being recognised.

So "everyone has ADHD now" isn't evidence of overdiagnosis. It's evidence that we spent a long time missing people who were quietly struggling.

Section 5: So here's exactly what to say back.

Let me give you the practical bit. Because you clicked this to feel less alone *and* to walk away with something useful.

Next time someone says "everyone's a little bit ADHD," you don't need to argue. You need one calm line.

[B-ROLL]

Try this: "Everyone has moments like that — the difference with ADHD is it's constant, it's across my whole life, and it genuinely costs me things other people don't lose."

Or shorter: "Feeling distracted sometimes is a trait. ADHD is a condition. Same word, totally different weight."

[B-ROLL]

And if they still don't get it? That's okay. You don't owe everyone a lecture. Your job isn't to convince the whole internet. Your job is to protect your own understanding of yourself — so their careless sentence doesn't quietly become the way you see yourself too.

Because that's the real damage of this phrase. Not the person saying it. The way it makes *us* doubt whether our struggle is even real.

It is real. You're not exaggerating. And you're not "a little bit" anything.
