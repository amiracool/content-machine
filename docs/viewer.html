POV: trying to explain to your anxiety that the email you sent was actually fine 😅

You hit send.

For one glorious second, you feel accomplished.

And then.

Your anxiety sits up. Cracks its knuckles. And says, *"So. Let's review."*

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever re-read a sent email seventeen times looking for a crime you didn't commit — stay right here. Because today we're sitting in the courtroom inside your brain. And spoiler: the prosecution never rests.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation.

You sent a normal email. To a normal person. About a normal thing.

Maybe it was your boss. Maybe a coworker. Maybe you just replied "sounds good!" to someone.

And logically? It was fine. It was *more* than fine. It was polite, clear, maybe even a little charming.

But your anxiety doesn't care about logic. Your anxiety is a defense attorney who got hired *after* the trial already started, and now it's panicking and throwing everything at the wall.

[B-ROLL]

So here's what I'm going to do.

I'm going to walk you through exactly what your anxious brain does to a perfectly good email — step by step. Because when you can *see* the pattern, you can start to interrupt it.

By the end of this, you'll have one real tool to shut down the mental courtroom before it wastes your entire evening.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: The Re-Read Spiral

The first thing your anxiety does is demand a recount.

You've read the email. You know what it says. You *wrote* it. But anxiety insists on reading it again — not for information, but for evidence.

It's like checking if you locked the door, except the door is a sentence that says "Let me know if you have any questions!"

[B-ROLL]

And every re-read makes it *worse*. Because the more you stare at a normal word, the weirder it looks. "Regards." Is "Regards" cold? Is "Regards" aggressive? Should it have been "Best"? Is "Best" better? Best at what?

Here's the truth: re-reading doesn't calm anxiety. It feeds it. You're giving a hungry worry more data to chew on.

So that's problem number one. Now watch what happens next.

Section 2: The Tone Investigation

This is where your brain becomes a forensic linguist.

Suddenly you're analyzing your own exclamation points like they're fingerprints at a crime scene.

[B-ROLL]

One exclamation point: "Okay, that's friendly, that's fine."

Zero exclamation points: "Oh no. Am I mad at them? Do they think I'm mad at them? Am I being passive aggressive?"

Three exclamation points: "Now I sound unhinged. I sound like I've had nine coffees. They're going to think something is deeply wrong with me."

[B-ROLL]

Here's what's wild. The person reading your email spent about four seconds on it. They are not doing tone analysis. They read "Thanks!" and thought, *cool, thanks.* And then they went to get a snack.

Your anxiety wrote an entire thriller novel about punctuation that the other person will never even open.

But your brain isn't done. Because now it's time for the worst part.

Section 3: The Imaginary Reply

This is the big one.

Your anxiety doesn't just worry about what you said. It writes the *other person's response* for them.

And it never writes a good one.

[B-ROLL]

You start imagining them reading your email, frowning, turning to a coworker and going, "Can you believe this person?" You build an entire scene. There's dialogue. There's a reaction shot. Someone's shaking their head.

You've essentially cast, directed, and starred in a short film called *They All Secretly Hate Me* — and you gave it a budget.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: your anxiety has a 100% conviction rate because it's playing *all* the roles. It's the prosecutor, the witness, the jury, AND the person who's supposedly mad at you. Of course it finds you guilty. It wrote everyone's lines.

[B-ROLL]

Real people are messy and distracted and thinking about their own emails. They are not building a case against you. They don't have time. They barely have time to answer the email at all.

So how do you actually get out of this? That's next.

Section 4: The "Future Fortune Teller" Trick

Okay. Here's the tool I promised you.

When your anxiety starts the trial, I want you to notice one specific thing: it's always talking about the *future.*

"They're *going* to think..." "They'll *probably* reply with..." "This is *going* to be awkward tomorrow."

[B-ROLL]

Your anxiety is pretending to be a witness, but it's actually a fortune teller. And it only predicts disasters.

So here's what you do. You catch the prediction, and you say out loud — actually out loud, it works better:

*"That's a prediction, not a fact."*

That's it. You don't have to argue with the anxiety. You don't have to prove the email was good. You just have to label the thought as what it is: a guess about a future that hasn't happened.

[B-ROLL]

Because the second you call it a prediction, something shifts. You're no longer trapped *inside* the courtroom. You're standing outside of it, looking in, going, "Oh. This again. This is just my brain doing the thing."

You don't have to win the case. You just have to leave the building.

Section 5: Let the Email Be Boring

Last thing. And this one's gentle.

The goal isn't to feel 100% certain the email was perfect. You'll probably never feel *certain.* That's not how anxiety works — it doesn't respond to reassurance. It just asks for more.

[B-ROLL]

The goal is to let the email be *boring.*

Not perfect. Not a disaster. Just... a normal email that you sent and will now stop thinking about. Mediocre. Forgettable. Fine.

[B-ROLL]

Because here's the secret your anxiety doesn't want you to know: most of the emails you've agonized over? You don't even remember them now. The other person *definitely* doesn't. They replied "sounds good" and moved on with their entire life.

Your anxiety spent three hours on a case that was already closed.

So let it be boring. Let it be done. The trial is over. You can go home.
