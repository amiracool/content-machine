# Every ADHD brain does THIS before starting a task (and hates it) 🧠

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

There's a task sitting in front of you right now.

It's simple. It'll take five minutes. You KNOW it'll take five minutes.

And yet... you've been staring at it for three hours.

You're not lazy. You're not broken. Your brain is doing something very specific before it lets you start — and once you see it, you'll never be able to unsee it.

[B-ROLL]

Every ADHD brain does THIS before starting a task. And I promise you — you hate it too.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you know way too well.

You want to do the thing. You genuinely want to. There's no part of you that enjoys sitting in the guilt of not doing it.

But between YOU and the task, there's this invisible wall. And no matter how hard you push, you can't seem to climb over it.

[B-ROLL]

So you end up doing everything EXCEPT the task. You reorganize your desk. You check your phone. You suddenly care deeply about a documentary on deep-sea fish.

[B-ROLL]

Here's what's actually happening. Before your brain will start a task, it runs a hidden process. Most people never notice it. But it's the reason "just start" advice never works for you.

In the next few minutes, I'm going to show you exactly what that process is, why it's happening, and three things you can do to shrink that wall down to a step you can actually walk over.

Stick with me. This one changes things.

BODY

Section 1: Your brain demands a "full simulation" before it moves

Before you start a task, your ADHD brain tries to simulate the ENTIRE thing from start to finish. Every step. Every possible problem. All at once.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like trying to watch every episode of a show at the same time, on the same screen, at full volume. It's overwhelming. So your brain does the logical thing — it hits pause and walks away.

That's not procrastination. That's your brain protecting itself from overload.

Here's the concrete example. Someone asks you to "reply to that email." Simple, right? But your brain doesn't hear "reply to email." It hears: find the email, remember the context, figure out the right tone, worry you'll say the wrong thing, imagine their reaction, wonder if you should've replied sooner, feel bad you didn't...

[B-ROLL]

By the time all that fires off, you're exhausted. And you haven't typed a single word.

So the fix isn't "try harder." The fix is starting BEFORE the simulation finishes. Which brings me to the next thing.

Section 2: You wait for the "right feeling" that never comes

Here's a lie your brain tells you constantly: "I'll do it when I feel ready."

[B-ROLL]

But for the ADHD brain, that ready feeling — that motivated, focused, "okay let's go" energy — often doesn't show up until AFTER you've already started. Not before.

Most people can borrow motivation from the future. They picture the reward, feel a little spark, and move. Our reward system runs differently. It's driven by urgency, novelty, and interest — not by "this is good for me in three weeks."

[B-ROLL]

So waiting to feel motivated is like standing next to a cold car waiting for it to warm up before you turn the key. It doesn't work that way. You turn the key first. The warmth comes after.

The concrete version? Commit to two minutes. Not the whole task. Just two. Tell yourself you can quit after. Nine times out of ten, once the engine's running, you keep driving.

[B-ROLL]

Because the momentum was never going to come first. You had to make it.

Which leads perfectly into the part almost nobody talks about.

Section 3: You're not avoiding the task — you're avoiding the FIRST STEP

This is the big one. Write it down if you have to.

You don't actually hate the task. You hate the fuzzy, undefined, "where do I even start" feeling that comes before it.

[B-ROLL]

When a task is vague, your brain can't grab onto it. "Clean the kitchen" isn't a task — it's a category. "Do my taxes" isn't a task — it's a nightmare with fifty hidden steps.

Here's the example. "Clean the kitchen" freezes you. But "put three cups in the sink" doesn't. That, you can do. And once you're standing there, cups in hand, the kitchen suddenly feels doable.

[B-ROLL]

The trick is to shrink the first step until it feels almost stupidly small. So small that saying no feels sillier than just doing it.

Your brain doesn't need the whole staircase lit up. It just needs to see the first step. Give it that, and it'll usually find the next one on its own.

Section 4: The guilt loop makes the wall taller

Here's the cruelest part of all this.

Every hour you don't start, the guilt grows. And that guilt doesn't push you toward the task. It pushes you AWAY from it.

[B-ROLL]

Because now the task isn't just a task. It's proof that something's wrong with you. It carries all the weight of "why can't I just be normal." And your brain will avoid emotional pain even harder than it avoids boring work.

Think of it like a snowball. The task itself is small. But roll it through a field of shame and self-criticism, and by the time you reach it, it's a boulder you can't move.

[B-ROLL]

The way out is counterintuitive. You have to drop the story that you're failing. You're not lazy. Your brain is genuinely wired to find task initiation harder — that's a real, documented part of ADHD, not a character flaw.

The kinder you are to yourself in this moment, the smaller the wall gets. Self-compassion isn't soft. For ADHD brains, it's strategy.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: Use your brain's weird fuel instead of fighting it

Last thing — and this one's fun.

Stop trying to run your ADHD brain on the same fuel everyone else uses. Discipline and willpower are premium gas your brain doesn't run on. But there are four fuels that actually work.

[B-ROLL]

Novelty — do the task in a new spot, with new music, a different pen. Trick the brain into thinking it's fresh.

Urgency — set a timer and race it. Genuinely try to beat the clock.

Interest — pair the boring task with something you like. A podcast, a favorite drink, body-doubling with a friend on a video call.

[B-ROLL]

Challenge — turn it into a game. How many emails in ten minutes? How fast can you fold this laundry?

The example that always lands: folding laundry feels impossible when it's a chore. But time yourself and try to beat yesterday's record, and suddenly you're weirdly into it.

[B-ROLL]

You're not lazy. You've just been trying to fuel a race car with the wrong gas. Change the fuel, and that same brain that couldn't start becomes unstoppable.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So here's the truth. The wall before every task isn't laziness. It's a brain running a hidden simulation, waiting for a feeling that never comes, freezing on a fuzzy first step, and buried under guilt it didn't deserve.

Now you can see it. And once you see it, you can shrink it.
