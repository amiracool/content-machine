# POV: Setting a Reminder With ADHD (And Ignoring It in Real Time)

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

POV: You just set the perfect reminder.

"Take out the trash — 7:00 PM."

You feel like a genius. You feel organized. You feel like the kind of person who has their life together.

[B-ROLL]

7:00 PM. The reminder goes off.

[B-ROLL]

And you swipe it away.

Not because you decided not to do it. Not because you forgot. You saw it. You read it. And your brain went, "Cool. Noted."

And then did absolutely nothing.

If this is you, keep watching. Because this isn't a willpower problem. It's a brain-wiring problem. And once you understand *why*, you can actually start working *with* it.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

[B-ROLL]

Here's the situation if you have ADHD.

You know what you need to do. You care about doing it. You set the reminder specifically so you wouldn't forget.

And then the reminder shows up... and it feels about as urgent as an ad for car insurance.

[B-ROLL]

You're not lazy. You're not irresponsible. You set the reminder! That's the frustrating part. You did the "right thing." But the reminder system was built for a brain that isn't yours.

So in this video, I'm going to break down three reasons your reminders don't work — and more importantly, four actual fixes that work *with* your ADHD brain instead of against it.

No "just try harder." No shame. Just stuff that actually sticks.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Your Brain Treats "Future You" Like a Stranger

Here's the first problem. When you set a reminder, you're making a deal with Future You.

"Hey Future Me, handle this at 7."

But Future You never agreed to that. Future You shows up at 7, exhausted, hyperfocused on something else, and goes, "Who scheduled this? Not me."

[B-ROLL]

ADHD affects something called *time blindness* and *prospective memory* — your ability to remember to do a thing in the future. For a lot of us, the future doesn't feel real. There's basically two time zones: now and not now.

[B-ROLL]

And the second that reminder isn't *now* anymore — the moment you swipe it — it instantly files itself under "not now." Forever.

It's like texting someone "I'll call you back" and then your brain deletes the whole conversation.

So the fix isn't a better reminder. The fix is making the task feel like it belongs to *now.* Hold that thought — we'll get there.

Section 2: The Notification Has No Teeth

Second reason. A reminder notification gives you zero stakes.

Think about it. What happens if you ignore it? Nothing. It just... goes away. There's no alarm screaming at you. No person waiting. No consequence in the next five seconds.

[B-ROLL]

ADHD brains run on urgency, interest, novelty, and challenge. A polite little banner that says "Take out the trash" hits exactly none of those.

It's like a smoke detector that whispers. Technically it's doing its job. But nobody's moving.

[B-ROLL]

Compare that to when you have a flight in 90 minutes and you haven't packed. *Suddenly* you're a productivity machine. You're folding clothes like a professional. Why? Urgency. Real stakes. A deadline with teeth.

[B-ROLL]

Your reminders fail because they have no teeth. So part of the fix is giving them some.

Section 3: You're Reminding, But You're Not *Capturing the Moment*

Here's the sneaky one. Timing.

Most people set a reminder for when they *want* to do the task. "Remind me to call the pharmacy at 2 PM."

But at 2 PM, you might be mid-hyperfocus, in the shower, driving, or deep in a completely different mission. The reminder interrupts you at the worst moment — so you swipe it to deal with "later." And later never files itself under *now.*

[B-ROLL]

The problem is you're reminding yourself based on *clock time* instead of *action time.*

Clock time: "at 2 PM." Action time: "when I get in the car" or "when I open my laptop."

[B-ROLL]

Your brain is way more likely to act when a reminder is attached to something you're *already doing.* Tie the new habit to an existing one. That's called habit stacking, and for ADHD brains, it's gold.

Okay. You know the three reasons. Now let's fix this.

Section 4: Make the Reminder Loud, Physical, and Impossible to Swipe

Fix number one: get the task out of your phone and into the real world.

A notification is easy to dismiss because it lives in the place where you dismiss a hundred things a day. So make it physical.

[B-ROLL]

Put the trash bag *in front of the door* so you physically have to move it. Set the pill bottle next to your coffee maker. Write it on a sticky note and slap it on the bathroom mirror.

[B-ROLL]

This works because now the reminder is *in your path.* You can't swipe a trash bag you're about to trip over.

And if you love alarms, name them. Don't let it say "Alarm." Make it say "GET UP, THE TRASH TRUCK IS COMING." Specific. Loud. A little dramatic. Your brain responds to drama.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: Do It Now, or Shrink It Until You Can

Fix number two — and this is the big one. When the reminder goes off, follow the two-minute rule: if it takes less than two minutes, do it *right now.*

Not later. Not after this episode. Now. Because "now" is the only time zone that works.

[B-ROLL]

But what if it's a big task? Then shrink it. The reminder shouldn't say "clean the kitchen." Your brain sees that and shuts down because it's huge and boring and shapeless.

[B-ROLL]

Make it tiny. "Put three dishes in the sink." That's it. Once you start, momentum usually carries you. And if it doesn't? You still put three dishes away. That's a win.

[B-ROLL]

The goal is to make the task smaller than your resistance to it.

Section 6: Add Stakes and a Witness

Fix number three: give your reminders teeth by adding stakes or accountability.

Remember how your brain moves mountains when there's real urgency? You can borrow that on purpose.

[B-ROLL]

Text a friend: "I'm going to send that email in 10 minutes, call me out if I don't." Suddenly there's a person. A witness. Stakes.

Or use *body doubling* — do the boring task while on a video call or in the same room as someone else, even if they're doing their own thing. The presence of another human turns "not now" into "oh, it's happening now."

[B-ROLL]

You can also gamify it. Race the microwave. Beat your own time. Reward yourself the second it's done. Your brain loves a challenge and loves a reward — so hand it both.

[B-ROLL]

Section 7: Stop Blaming Yourself for a Tool That Doesn't Fit

And fix number four is honestly the most important one. Stop interpreting a swiped reminder as proof that you're failing.

[B-ROLL]

You are not broken. Your reminder system was designed for a brain that files the future under "real." Yours files it under "not now." That's not a character flaw — it's wiring.

So when a reminder doesn't work, you don't need more guilt. You need a different tool. Try the sticky note. Try the two-minute rule. Try the friend. Keep the ones that stick, drop the ones that don't.

[B-ROLL]

The people who seem "organized" aren't more disciplined than you. They've just found systems that match their brain. You're allowed to do the same.
