# Making a To-Do List but Your ADHD Already Forgot Step One 📝🤯

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

You sit down. Fresh notebook. Brand new pen.

Today is the day you finally get organized.

You write "1." at the top of the page.

And then... nothing.

Your brain, which had seventeen urgent tasks thirty seconds ago, is now completely empty.

[B-ROLL]

What was step one? You literally JUST knew. It was right there.

If this is you — welcome. You're not lazy. You're not broken. Your working memory just rage-quit.

And by the end of this video, you're going to understand exactly why this happens — and what to actually do about it.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you about ADHD and to-do lists.

The problem isn't that you're disorganized. The problem is that the standard advice — "just make a list!" — was designed for brains that work completely differently from yours.

[B-ROLL]

For a neurotypical brain, a to-do list is a tool. For an ADHD brain, a blank to-do list can feel like a trap.

You know you have things to do. You can FEEL the weight of them. But the second you try to pin one down, it slips away. Then you panic. Then you open your phone "just to check something." And now it's forty minutes later and you're watching a video about ADHD and to-do lists.

[B-ROLL]

Hi. Yeah. We're in it together.

So here's what I'm going to give you. Three reasons your lists fail — and three fixes that actually match how your brain works. No shame. No "just try harder." Let's go.

---

BODY

Section 1: Your working memory is a leaky bucket

Here's what's actually happening when you forget step one.

ADHD affects working memory — the mental sticky note that holds information while you use it. For you, that sticky note doesn't stick. It falls off the second you look away.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like trying to carry water in your hands. You can hold it for a second. But by the time you walk to the sink, half of it's gone — and you're not sure where it went.

So when you sit down to write a list, you're asking your leakiest system to do the heaviest lifting. Of course it drops the ball.

The fix: Don't write your list from memory. Capture tasks the MOMENT you think of them. Voice note. Phone reminder. Whatever's closest.

[B-ROLL]

The task you remember at 2pm is gone by 2:05. Catch it in the air. Don't wait for the notebook.

Okay — but even when you DO write things down, there's a second trap waiting for you.

Section 2: Your list is too big to feel real

Let me guess. Your to-do list says things like "clean the house." "Do taxes." "Sort out my whole life."

[B-ROLL]

Those aren't tasks. Those are mountains. And your ADHD brain looks at a mountain and does one of two things: panics, or freezes.

Here's why. The ADHD brain struggles with task initiation — the actual act of starting. And a vague, giant task gives your brain nowhere to grip. There's no obvious first move, so it stalls out.

It's like being told "go run a marathon" when you can't even find your shoes.

[B-ROLL]

The fix: Shrink the task until it feels almost stupidly small. Not "clean the kitchen." Try "put ONE dish in the sink."

Make the first step so tiny your brain can't argue with it. Because once you start — once you get a little hit of momentum — the rest often follows. Starting is the hard part. So make starting ridiculously easy.

[B-ROLL]

And if even that feels too hard? That's not a willpower problem. That's a brain-chemistry problem. Which brings me to the big one.

Section 3: Your list has no urgency and no reward

Here's the uncomfortable truth. The ADHD brain is interest-based, not importance-based.

[B-ROLL]

That means your brain won't release the motivation chemicals you need — dopamine — just because something is *important*. It needs one of four things: urgency, novelty, interest, or challenge.

So your tidy little list of important-but-boring tasks? It's basically invisible to your motivation system. Your brain scrolls right past it like an ad.

That's why you can clean your entire apartment at 11pm the night before guests arrive — but can't do one dish on a calm Tuesday. The 11pm version has URGENCY. The Tuesday version has nothing.

[B-ROLL]

The fix: Build in the chemicals your brain is craving. Add urgency with a timer — "I'll do this for five minutes only." Add novelty by changing where or how you work. Add reward by stacking a task you hate onto something you love. Fold laundry while your favorite show plays.

[B-ROLL]

You're not forcing yourself to care. You're giving your brain a reason it can actually feel.

Section 4: You're treating the list like a test you can fail

This is the one that quietly breaks people.

Somewhere along the way, your to-do list stopped being a helper and became a scorecard. Every unchecked box is proof you're failing. So looking at the list makes you feel bad. And when something makes you feel bad, your brain avoids it.

[B-ROLL]

So now you avoid the very tool that's supposed to help you. The list causes shame, the shame causes avoidance, the avoidance causes more unfinished tasks. It's a loop. And you've probably been stuck in it for years.

The fix: Change the rules of the game. Make a "done list" instead of a to-do list. At the end of the day, write down what you ACTUALLY did — including the invisible stuff. Replied to that scary email. Took a shower. Kept a human alive. You.

[B-ROLL]

Your brain runs on dopamine. Checking things off gives you dopamine. So give yourself the win, even for things you already finished. You're not cheating. You're working *with* your wiring instead of against it.

You never had a discipline problem. You had a system that was built for a brain that isn't yours.
