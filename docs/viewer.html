# POV: Your Brain During a Panic Attack vs. What Your Face Is Showing 😐

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

On the outside, I look... fine.

I'm sitting in this meeting. Nodding. Maybe half-smiling.

[B-ROLL]

But inside my head?

[B-ROLL]

Inside my head, the building is on fire, my heart is trying to escape through my ribs, and a voice is screaming *you are going to die in this Tuesday sync.*

And nobody in this room has any idea.

Because that's the part nobody tells you about panic attacks. Most of them are silent. Invisible. Happening two feet away from people who think you're just... a little quiet today.

Let me show you what's actually going on.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

If you've ever had a panic attack in public and thought, *why does no one notice I'm dying right now* — this one's for you.

Here's the problem. When people picture a panic attack, they picture the movie version. Someone hyperventilating on the floor. Hands shaking. Crying. Loud.

[B-ROLL]

But for a lot of us? It's the opposite.

We freeze. We go quiet. We get *really* good at looking normal — because some part of us is terrified of being seen falling apart.

So you end up doing two full-time jobs at once. Job one: surviving the panic. Job two: hiding the panic.

And that second job is exhausting. Nobody claps for it because nobody knows you're doing it.

So here's what I want to give you today. I'm going to break down the *real* gap between what your brain is doing and what your face is showing. Because once you can name it, it loses some of its power. And I'll leave you with something you can actually do in the moment.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: The face is lying, and it's lying for a reason

The gap between your inside and your outside during a panic attack isn't fake. It's a defense mechanism.

Your face going neutral isn't you "being dramatic for attention." It's the exact opposite. Your nervous system decided the safest move is to disappear. To not make a scene. To not be a burden.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a swan. On the surface, gliding, calm, elegant. Under the water, the legs are paddling like their life depends on it.

[B-ROLL]

That's you in the meeting. That's you at the dinner table. That's you in the grocery checkout line while the panic climbs your spine and you're doing math about whether you can make it to the car.

The reason this matters: you are not "overreacting." Your body is running an old survival program at full volume, and you're the only one who can hear the alarm. That's not weakness. That's a very tired person doing something incredibly hard, quietly.

So if the face is the lie — let's talk about what's actually happening underneath.

Section 2: Your brain isn't broken, it's misfiring the smoke alarm

Here's the thing that helped me the most. During a panic attack, your brain is not malfunctioning. It's doing its number one job — keeping you alive — just at the wrong time.

There's a part of your brain called the amygdala. Think of it as a smoke detector.

[B-ROLL]

A smoke detector doesn't think. It doesn't ask, *is this a real fire, or did somebody just make toast?* It just screams. That's its whole job.

[B-ROLL]

During a panic attack, your smoke detector goes off over burnt toast. There's no actual threat. No lion. No danger. But your body doesn't get the memo. It floods you with adrenaline like you're about to sprint away from something.

So your heart pounds — that's your body preparing to run. Your chest gets tight — that's your breathing speeding up for a fight that isn't coming. You feel dizzy or unreal — that's your brain rerouting resources away from "thinking" toward "surviving."

[B-ROLL]

None of that is you losing your mind. It's a chemical reaction with a beginning, a middle, and — this is the important part — an end. Adrenaline burns off. It cannot last forever. Your body physically can't keep it going.

Knowing that? Changes everything about how you ride it out. Which brings me to the next part.

Section 3: The silent panic tax nobody talks about

There's a cost to being the person who always "seems fine." I call it the silent panic tax.

Because when you're an expert at hiding it, people stop checking on you. They see the calm swan. They never see the paddling. So you learn, over and over, that no one's coming — which makes the next one lonelier.

[B-ROLL]

And then afterward comes the crash. You get home, the panic finally drains out, and you feel wiped. Foggy. A little ashamed of something that wasn't your fault. You might replay it: *did anyone notice? was I weird? why can't I just be normal?*

That replay loop is its own tax. You paid to survive the panic, and then you pay again in the shame afterward.

[B-ROLL]

Here's what I need you to hear. You do not owe anyone a performance of being okay. And the reason you got so good at hiding is that at some point, hiding kept you safe. That was smart. That protected you.

But you're allowed to put the tax down now. You're allowed to tell one person. You're allowed to leave the room. You're allowed to let the swan legs show, just a little.

So let's talk about what you can actually do when it's happening.

Section 4: The 4-second reset you can do without anyone noticing

Since so much of this happens in public, you need tools that are invisible. Nobody wants a "just breathe" lecture in the middle of a work call. So here are three you can do while still looking like a calm human.

One: the long exhale. Forget the deep breath in — that can actually make panic worse. The magic is in the *out*. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six.

[B-ROLL]

Why it works: a long exhale is a direct message to your nervous system that says *the danger is over.* It's one of the only ways to talk to your body in a language it actually listens to. And you can do it silently under a table.

Two: name five things you can see. Out loud in your head, not the room. The chair. The window. The pen. The mug. The exit sign.

[B-ROLL]

Why it works: panic lives in the future — *what if, what if, what if.* Naming what's in front of you drags your brain back into the present, where the fire actually isn't.

Three: cold. Hold something cold. A water bottle. Splash your wrists in the bathroom. Press your feet hard into the floor.

[B-ROLL]

Why it works: a sharp physical sensation gives your overloaded brain one single thing to focus on, and cold specifically helps slow the heart rate. It's a reset button you carry everywhere.

You don't need all three. Pick one. Practice it *before* you need it, so your body already knows the drill when the smoke detector goes off.

Section 5: What to say if you want to stop hiding

And if you're ready to let one person in — you don't need a big speech.

[B-ROLL]

You can just say: *"Hey. Sometimes I have panic attacks and they're mostly invisible. If I go quiet or need to step out, that's what's happening. You don't have to fix it. Just don't take it personally."*

[B-ROLL]

That's it. That one sentence turns you from the lonely swan into a person with backup. And the relief of not performing anymore? That's a tax refund you didn't know you had coming.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So the next time your face says *fine* and your brain says *fire* — remember. Your body isn't betraying you. It's protecting you with old software. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end. And you are not the only one sitting in that meeting quietly surviving.
