# 6 Weird Hacks to Actually Fall Asleep When Your ADHD Brain Won't Shut Up at 2am 😴🧠

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

It's 2am. You have to be up in five hours. And your brain just decided *now* is the perfect time to replay that cringey thing you said in 2014.

Sound familiar?

[B-ROLL]

Here's the thing nobody tells you. ADHD doesn't clock out at bedtime. Your brain is still sprinting while your body is begging to stop.

So tonight, I'm giving you 6 genuinely weird hacks that work *with* your brain instead of against it. No "just meditate" nonsense. These are the actual tricks that get a racing ADHD brain to finally power down.

Stick around for number 4. It feels backwards, but it's the one that changed everything for me.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Let's name what's really happening.

You're not bad at sleeping. You're not broken. Your brain just doesn't produce dopamine and shut-off signals the way other people's do.

[B-ROLL]

So when the room goes dark and quiet, your brain panics. No stimulation? Cool, it'll make its own. Hello, random worries. Hello, business ideas at midnight. Hello, that song you haven't heard in ten years on infinite loop.

[B-ROLL]

Regular sleep advice assumes a brain that *wants* to slow down. Yours doesn't. It needs to be gently tricked.

So here's what I'm promising you. By the end of this video, you'll have six tools you can try *tonight*. Not someday. Tonight. And at least one of them is going to click for your specific brain.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: Give your brain a boring job

Your ADHD brain hates nothing more than *nothing*. Silence is an invitation to spiral. So don't fight the noise. Replace it with something so dull your brain gives up.

Think of it like handing a toddler a quiet toy so they stop running around the house.

[B-ROLL]

The trick is *boring on purpose*. A sleep story. A documentary you've already seen. A podcast about something you mildly care about. Just enough to occupy the chatty part of your mind, not enough to make you want to stay awake.

I personally use the same 40-minute history podcast every single night. I've never heard the ending. That's the point.

And once your brain has its boring job, we can work on your body.

Section 2: The "put your racing thoughts somewhere" dump

If your brain won't stop listing things, stop trying to hold them in your head. Get them out.

It's like your mind is a browser with 47 tabs open. You can't close them by ignoring them. You have to actually click the X.

[B-ROLL]

Keep a notepad by your bed. When a thought grabs you — a task, a worry, a brilliant 2am idea — write one line and let it go. You're telling your brain, "I got it. It's saved. You can stop reminding me."

This works because the worry loop is your brain's way of making sure you don't forget. Once it's on paper, the loop has nowhere to go.

Pen and paper beats your phone here, by the way. Which brings me to the next one.

Section 3: Trap your phone across the room

This one hurts. I know.

Your phone is dopamine on demand. And an under-stimulated ADHD brain will reach for it every single time. One "quick check" turns into two hours of scrolling and sudden wide-awake panic.

[B-ROLL]

So make it physically annoying to get to. Charge it across the room. Or in another room entirely. The friction of having to actually stand up is usually enough to stop you.

Think of it like keeping snacks out of the house. You're not relying on willpower you don't have. You're removing the option.

If you need an alarm, buy a five-dollar alarm clock. Yes, really. Your sleep is worth more than five bucks.

Okay. Here's the one I told you to wait for.

Section 4: Get OUT of bed when you can't sleep

I know. This sounds completely backwards.

But lying in bed awake for an hour teaches your brain that bed equals *frustration*. You're accidentally training yourself to feel anxious the second your head hits the pillow.

[B-ROLL]

So if you've been lying there spiraling for more than about twenty minutes, get up. Go somewhere dim. Do something low-key and boring. Read a few pages. Fold laundry. Just nothing stimulating, nothing bright.

When you feel genuinely sleepy, go back. You're protecting the one thing that matters most: your bed being a place for sleep, not stress.

It feels counterintuitive. But it rewires the whole association. Trust me on this one.

Section 5: Cool down your body on purpose

Your body needs to drop in temperature to fall asleep. And ADHD folks often run warm, wired, and restless. So we hack it from the outside.

[B-ROLL]

Simple stuff. Crack a window. Run a fan. Stick one leg out of the covers — that sock-off, leg-out move is actually your body regulating heat. A cool room signals "it's time."

Think of it like your body needs a runway to land on. Too hot and it just keeps circling.

Some people swear by a warm shower an hour before bed, because the drop in temperature afterward makes you drowsy. Experiment. Your brain, your rules.

One more. And it's the one most people skip.

Section 6: Stop trying so hard to fall asleep

Here's the paradox. The more desperately you try to sleep, the more awake you get.

Because trying is effort. And effort is stimulation. Your brain can't relax while it's performing the task of relaxing.

[B-ROLL]

So flip it. Tell yourself you're just going to rest. You don't *have* to sleep. You're just lying here, letting your body be heavy, no pressure, no deadline.

It's like trying to remember a word. The harder you chase it, the further it runs. Let go, and it shows up on its own.

Lower the stakes and your nervous system finally gets the message that it's safe to drift.
