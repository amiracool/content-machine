# When Someone With Anxiety Tries to Send One Simple Text 😩

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When someone with anxiety tries to send one simple text.

Watch this.

*"Hey, are we still on for Saturday?"*

That's it. That's the whole message. Seven words.

[B-ROLL]

But you don't hit send. You stare at it.

Is "Hey" too casual? Too cold? Does the question mark sound demanding? Should you add an emoji so they know you're friendly? But which one? Not that one, that's weird.

[B-ROLL]

Twenty minutes go by. You've rewritten this text nine times. Your heart is racing over a message about *brunch.*

If you know this feeling in your bones — keep watching. Because I'm going to show you exactly what's happening in your brain, and how to actually hit send.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody tells you.

If you have anxiety, sending a text isn't sending a text. It's running a full risk assessment on a relationship, your worth, and every possible way this could go wrong.

[B-ROLL]

You're not being dramatic. You're not "overthinking it." Your brain is genuinely treating a low-stakes message like it's a life-or-death situation. Because to your nervous system? It kind of is.

And the worst part is the shame afterward. You watch other people fire off texts without a second thought, and you wonder what's wrong with you.

Nothing is wrong with you.

So here's what I'm giving you in the next few minutes. First, I'll show you what's *actually* happening in the anxious brain when you try to send that text. Then I'll give you three real strategies that help you hit send without the 20-minute spiral.

By the end of this, that "are we still on for Saturday?" text is going out the door. Let's go.

---

BODY

Section 1: Your brain isn't reading the text — it's reading danger

Here's what most people don't understand. When you type that message, your anxious brain isn't focused on the words. It's scanning for threat.

Think of it like a smoke detector that goes off when you make toast. The alarm is working. It's just way too sensitive. Your brain sees a tiny social risk and blasts the same alarm it would for actual danger.

[B-ROLL]

So when you read your own text back, you're not asking "is this clear?" You're asking "will this make them leave, judge me, or think I'm annoying?" That's a completely different question. And it's exhausting.

[B-ROLL]

Once you know the alarm is oversensitive, you can stop trusting it blindly. That's step one. Now let's talk about *why* the rewriting never ends.

Section 2: The "perfect version" doesn't exist — and your brain knows it

You keep rewriting because part of you believes there's a perfect version of this text. One that guarantees they won't be annoyed, won't misread you, won't like you less.

Spoiler: that version doesn't exist. You could write it a hundred ways and your brain would still find a flaw. That's not a message problem. That's an anxiety loop.

[B-ROLL]

It's like trying to fold a fitted sheet perfectly. You keep adjusting and readjusting, but there is no clean way to do it. At some point you just have to shove it in the closet and close the door.

[B-ROLL]

The goal isn't the perfect text. The goal is a *good enough* text that gets sent. Because an unsent perfect message helps nobody. And here's how you actually get there.

Section 3: The 3-second rule (send it before the spiral loads)

Okay, first real strategy. The spiral takes a few seconds to fully load. Your first instinct is usually fine. It's the second-guessing that ruins it.

So here's the move. Type the text like you're talking to a friend. Then count down from three and hit send *before* your brain has time to boot up the interrogation.

[B-ROLL]

Three. Two. One. Gone. No re-reading. No "let me just check it one more time." That last check is where you fall in.

I know it feels reckless. That's the point. You're teaching your brain that sending an imperfect text does not end your life. Every time you do it and nothing bad happens, the alarm gets a little quieter.

[B-ROLL]

Start small. Practice on low-stakes people. Then work your way up. Speaking of low stakes — the next trick is about lowering the pressure entirely.

Section 4: Lower the stakes by writing it "wrong" on purpose

This one sounds backwards, but stay with me. A lot of your anxiety comes from believing every word carries huge weight. So flood the zone with tiny "imperfections" until they stop scaring you.

Send the casual "hey." Use the lowercase. Leave out the perfectly balanced emoji. Let a text be a little messy on purpose.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the truth. Real people send messy texts constantly. Your friends do. And you never sit there analyzing *their* punctuation. You just read the meaning and move on. That's exactly what they're doing with yours.

[B-ROLL]

You are holding yourself to a standard nobody else even notices. Let the text be human. Because now we need to deal with the hardest part — the wait.

Section 5: The waiting game — what to do after you hit send

Sending the text isn't the finish line. Then comes the silence. The three dots that appear and disappear. The "they read it 40 minutes ago" panic.

Here's the reframe. Someone not replying instantly is almost never about you. People get busy. Phones get put down. A slow reply is not a verdict on your worth.

[B-ROLL]

So the strategy is simple. Send it, then physically remove yourself from the phone. Go do one small thing. Wash a mug. Step outside. Give your nervous system a job that isn't refreshing the chat.

[B-ROLL]

Because ninety percent of the time? The reply is warm and totally normal. And you spent all that energy bracing for a rejection that was never coming.

That's the pattern anxiety runs. It makes you pay in advance for something that usually never happens.
