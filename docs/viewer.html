# When ADHD Brains Try to Fall Asleep at a Normal Hour 🌙

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When ADHD brains try to fall asleep at a normal hour... 🌙

It's 10 PM. You're in bed. Lights off. Perfect.

And then your brain goes: "Hey. Remember that embarrassing thing you said in 2014?"

[B-ROLL]

You didn't ask for this meeting. But apparently your brain scheduled one. Full agenda. No end time. And you're the only one who showed up.

If you've ever laid perfectly still, exhausted, *begging* your body to sleep while your mind throws a whole festival — keep watching. Because this isn't a willpower problem. And I'm going to show you exactly why.

[B-ROLL]

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you know too well.

You're tired all day. Genuinely tired. You could fall asleep at your desk at 2 PM no problem.

But the moment your head hits the pillow? Suddenly you're wide awake. Wired. Planning your entire life. Solving problems you don't even have yet.

[B-ROLL]

This is one of the most common ADHD struggles nobody warns you about. It's called "delayed sleep phase," and it hits neurodivergent brains hard.

The problem isn't that you can't sleep. It's that nobody ever explained *why* your brain does this. So you blame yourself. You think you're broken. You think you just have bad discipline.

You don't.

By the end of this video, you'll understand three specific reasons ADHD makes falling asleep feel impossible — and one shift that actually helps. No "just put your phone away" advice. Real stuff.

[B-ROLL]

---

BODY

Section 1: Your brain never got the "off" signal

Most brains slow down at night. The mental noise fades. ADHD brains? They don't have that dimmer switch. They have an on/off switch that's stuck on "on."

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a browser with 47 tabs open. During the day, work and noise keep most tabs hidden behind the one you're using. But at night, when everything goes quiet? All 47 tabs start playing audio at once.

That random argument from three days ago. A song you heard once. Your grocery list. Whether whales have belly buttons. All of it. At full volume.

[B-ROLL]

This is because ADHD affects how your brain regulates attention and stimulation. Quiet isn't calming for you — it's a vacuum. And your brain rushes to fill it.

So when you can't "just relax and fall asleep," it's not because you're doing bedtime wrong. It's because your brain literally struggles to power down. Which brings me to the next thing making it worse.

Section 2: You're chasing the dopamine you never got

Here's the sneaky one. ADHD brains run low on dopamine — that feel-good, motivation chemical. And late at night is often when you finally get some.

[B-ROLL]

Ever notice you get a weird second wind around 11 PM? Suddenly you want to start a project. Reorganize your closet. Learn a new language. Watch one more video.

That's not random. That's your brain going: "Finally. No pressure. No tasks. I can actually enjoy something." So it grabs stimulation and refuses to let go. Sleep feels like *losing* the fun.

[B-ROLL]

We call it "revenge bedtime procrastination." You stay up not because you're not tired — but because your day felt controlled by everyone else. And these late hours are the only time that feels like yours.

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever stayed up scrolling knowing you'll regret it tomorrow — and done it anyway — that's this. It's not weakness. It's a brain trying to steal back a little joy. But there's one more piece.

Section 3: Your body clock is set to a different timezone

Here's the part that finally made me feel *seen.*

Research shows a lot of ADHD folks have a naturally delayed circadian rhythm. In plain English? Your internal clock is set later than everyone else's.

[B-ROLL]

So when society says "sleep at 10," your body genuinely thinks it's still early evening. You're not failing to fall asleep. Your biology hasn't clocked in for bedtime yet.

It's like being forced to sleep according to a timezone you don't live in. Imagine someone telling a person in New York to fall asleep on Tokyo time. They'd lie there wide awake too. That's not a character flaw. That's geography.

[B-ROLL]

This is why "just go to bed earlier" advice fails you. It's fighting your actual biology. And no amount of willpower beats your body's internal clock.

So what actually helps? Let's talk about the one shift that matters.

Section 4: The fix isn't "try harder" — it's "wind down louder"

Everyone tells you to relax before bed. Dim lights. Quiet room. Nothing.

But we just learned quiet is a *vacuum* for your brain. So doing "nothing" backfires.

[B-ROLL]

The real fix? Give your brain a low-stimulation *anchor.* Not nothing — something small and boring that occupies just enough of your mind so the 47 tabs quiet down.

A boring audiobook you've heard before. A calm podcast. A sleep story. Even a fan for background noise. You're not trying to force silence. You're giving your busy brain a soft place to land.

[B-ROLL]

And for the body clock? Get sunlight in your eyes first thing in the morning. Ten minutes. It nudges your internal clock earlier over time — gently, biologically. Way more effective than staring at the ceiling at midnight.

[B-ROLL]

The goal isn't to force an ADHD brain into a non-ADHD schedule. It's to work *with* how your brain actually runs.

[B-ROLL]

Because the whole time you thought you were bad at sleeping? You were just given rules built for a brain that isn't yours.
