# POV: Trying to Give Someone With Autism a "Quick Verbal Update" 📋

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"Hey, quick update before you head out—"

*Screen freezes. Record scratch.*

Watch what actually just happened inside their brain.

You said "quick." They heard: *a wall of sound with no beginning, no end, and no way to catch it before it's gone.*

You think you gave them three simple points.

They got zero. Because three simple points, said out loud, in a hallway, while you're already walking away… is not simple at all.

[B-ROLL]

Let me show you exactly what a "quick verbal update" feels like on the other side—and how to actually get through.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation. You're a manager, a partner, a friend, a coworker.

You mean well. You're busy. So you do the efficient thing—you talk.

You catch someone in the hallway. You rattle off three changes to the plan. You say "sound good?" and they nod.

And then… nothing happens. Or the wrong thing happens.

[B-ROLL]

And now you're frustrated. You think they weren't listening. They think they failed you. Again.

Here's the truth nobody tells you: for a lot of autistic people, verbal-only information isn't a smaller version of written information. It's a completely different, much harder format.

By the end of this video, you'll understand why the "quick verbal update" is basically the hardest way to communicate with them—and you'll have three swaps that make you the person they can actually work with.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: "Quick" and "verbal" is the worst combination

Speech disappears the moment it's spoken. There's no rewind button on a hallway conversation.

For a lot of autistic people, processing spoken language takes an extra beat. Not because they're slow—their brain is doing more work per word. It's translating tone, filtering background noise, and organizing the information all at once.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like this. Written info is a document you can reread. Verbal info is a train that only stops once. Blink and it's gone.

So when you say "quick," you're actually speeding up the one train they needed to run slow.

By the time they've fully processed point one, you're already on point three—and points two and three just left the station.

[B-ROLL]

That's not a listening problem. That's a format problem. Which means it's a fixable problem.

Section 2: The nod is not a "yes"

Here's the part that trips everyone up. They nodded. So you assume they got it.

But that nod often means: *"I hear that you are talking and I don't want to interrupt or seem difficult."*

[B-ROLL]

A lot of autistic people mask hard in the moment. Asking you to slow down, repeat, or write it down feels risky. Like admitting failure. Like being "too much."

So they nod. They fill in the gaps later. Sometimes they guess. And guessing on a work update goes exactly how you'd expect.

Think of the nod like an autopay you forgot to cancel. It looks like everything's fine on the surface. Then the bill arrives and it's a disaster.

[B-ROLL]

So don't read the nod as confirmation. The real test isn't "do they seem like they got it." It's "can they tell it back to me, or do they have it written somewhere they can check?"

That distinction changes everything.

Section 3: Swap 1 — Send it, then say it

Here's your first practical fix. Flip the order.

Don't verbalize and then maybe follow up in writing. Send the update in writing first. A message, an email, a shared note. Then, if you want, talk about it.

[B-ROLL]

Now the spoken part isn't the source of truth. It's just backup. If they miss a word, no problem—it's all right there, in a format they can reread as many times as they need.

Think of it like giving someone directions. You wouldn't shout street names out a car window as they drive off. You'd drop a pin.

[B-ROLL]

Written first, spoken second. It sounds like more work. It's actually less—because you only have to say it once, and it actually lands.

Section 4: Swap 2 — Number it and end it clearly

A "quick update" usually has no shape. It starts mid-thought and trails off into "yeah, so, anyway."

Give it structure instead. Say how many points there are before you start. "Three things." Then number them. Then name the action.

[B-ROLL]

"Three things. One, the meeting moved to Thursday. Two, I need the draft by end of day. Three, ignore the old file. What I need from you: send me the draft Thursday morning."

See the difference? Now their brain has a container to pour the information into. It knows when a point starts, when it ends, and what to do with it.

Vague instructions are like handing someone a pile of loose puzzle pieces and no box. Numbered points are the box lid with the picture on it.

[B-ROLL]

And always—always—end with "what I need from you." That's the part people skip, and it's the part that matters most.

Section 5: Swap 3 — Replace "sound good?" with a real check

"Sound good?" only ever gets one answer. Yes. It's a trap disguised as a question.

Instead, ask something that requires them to actually hold the information.

[B-ROLL]

Try: "Just so I know I explained it clearly—what's the first thing you'll do?"

Notice what that does. You put the pressure on your explanation, not on their comprehension. You made it safe to say "wait, run that by me again." You took away the shame.

[B-ROLL]

Because here's the thing—a lot of the breakdown isn't about ability. It's about safety. When it's safe to ask, people ask. When it's not, they nod and guess.

This isn't babying anyone. This is just good communication. The wild part? Every single one of these swaps helps everyone. The distracted person. The person in a noisy room. The one who's exhausted. You're not lowering the bar—you're building a ramp everyone can use.

[B-ROLL]

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So next time you're about to give a "quick verbal update," remember: quick and verbal is the hardest combo there is.

Send it, then say it. Number it and end it. And ditch "sound good?" for a real check.
