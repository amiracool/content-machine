# My Response to the Article Claiming ADHD Medication "Changes Who You Are"

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

Someone sent me an article this week. The headline said ADHD medication "changes who you are."

And I felt my stomach drop. Because I remember reading something exactly like that before I got treated. It scared me off medication for three more years.

Three years of my life. Gone. Because of a headline written by someone who has never had this brain.

So today, I'm going to tell you the truth about what medication actually does. Not the fear. Not the myth. The truth. And by the end, you'll understand your own brain better than that article ever could.

[B-ROLL]

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's who I'm talking to.

You've been wondering if medication could help. Maybe your doctor mentioned it. Maybe a friend takes it and swears by it. But every time you get close to trying it, you hear that voice.

"What if it changes me?"

"What if I lose my personality?"

"What if the funny, creative, spontaneous parts of me just... disappear?"

I know that fear because I lived inside it. For years.

[B-ROLL]

So here's what I'm going to give you. I'm going to explain what medication does at the level of your actual brain. I'm going to separate the real side effects from the internet horror stories. And I'm going to help you tell the difference between "changing who you are" and finally becoming who you already were.

Because those are not the same thing. Not even close.

Let's get into it.

---

BODY

Section 1: Medication doesn't add a personality. It removes the noise.

The biggest myth in that article is the idea that medication puts something *into* you. Like it installs a new operating system.

That's backwards.

Think of it like this. Imagine you're trying to have a conversation at a party. But there are twelve other conversations happening around you, all at the same volume. A song you love keeps pulling your attention. Someone's laughing behind you. You're trying to focus, but your brain treats every single sound as equally important.

That's the ADHD brain without treatment. Everything is turned up to the same level. The important thing and the random thing get the exact same spotlight.

Medication doesn't add a new conversation. It just turns down the twelve you didn't ask for.

[B-ROLL]

You're still you. Your sense of humor is still there. Your ideas are still there. You can just finally *hear yourself think* over all the static.

That's not a new personality. That's your real one, finally getting through.

Which brings me to the thing people confuse it with.

Section 2: What people call "personality change" is usually the crash — or the wrong dose.

Okay, I'll be honest with you. Because I hate when creators pretend medication is magic with zero downsides.

Some people *do* feel off on medication. Flat. Robotic. Not themselves.

But here's what that article won't tell you. That feeling almost always means one of two things. The dose is too high. Or it's the wrong medication for your brain.

[B-ROLL]

Stimulant medication isn't one thing. There are different types, different release speeds, different doses. Getting it right is a process, not a coin flip.

Think of it like getting glasses. If someone hands you a random pair off the shelf, the world looks worse, not better. Everything's blurry and your head hurts. You wouldn't say "glasses ruin your vision." You'd say "those were the wrong glasses."

[B-ROLL]

There's also the crash. When some medications wear off in the afternoon, you can feel irritable or drained. That's not who you are. That's a timing problem, and it's fixable.

So when someone says "medication made me a zombie," what they're usually describing is a dose or a match that was never dialed in. That's a conversation with your doctor. Not a reason to quit before you start.

Section 3: The "who you are" you're afraid of losing was built by struggle.

This one is deeper. And I need you to sit with it.

A lot of us have a personality that was shaped by having an untreated brain. We became the person who cracks jokes to distract from the fact we didn't do the reading. The person who's chronically late but "chill about it." The person who takes on ten projects because saying no feels impossible.

We start to think that *is* us.

[B-ROLL]

But some of that isn't personality. It's coping. It's the armor you built to survive a world that wasn't designed for your brain.

And when medication quiets the chaos, some of that armor comes off. That can feel weird. Even sad. You might think, "Wait, where did that part of me go?"

But ask yourself this. Was that part of you actually you? Or was it a bandage over a wound?

[B-ROLL]

The impulsivity that got you into trouble isn't your soul. Your creativity, your empathy, your quick pattern-brain — that stays. What often leaves is the suffering you'd wrapped around your identity because you had no other choice.

Losing that isn't losing yourself. It's meeting yourself without the fight.

Section 4: The real question isn't "will it change me" — it's "which version of me gets to show up."

Here's the reframe that changed everything for me.

Every day you're already deciding who shows up. Untreated, I showed up as the version of me who forgot my best friend's birthday. Who started strong and finished nothing. Who lay awake at 2 a.m. hating myself for a day I couldn't seem to control.

[B-ROLL]

That was also "who I am." Nobody worries about *that* version disappearing.

When I finally got the right treatment, a different version got to show up. The one who could actually finish the sentence he started. Who could listen to his partner without three tabs open in his head. Who could sit down and do the thing he cared about.

[B-ROLL]

Same values. Same jokes. Same weird obsessions. Just present.

That article treats "change" like it's automatically bad. But you change every time you sleep well. Every time you eat. Every time you go to therapy. Growth is change. The goal was never to freeze exactly who you are while you're drowning.

The goal is to give the best version of you a fighting chance to be in the room.

Section 5: Medication is a tool, not an identity — and you're always the one holding it.

Last thing, because I don't want you to swing too far the other way.

Medication is not a personality transplant, and it's also not a cure-all. It's a tool. A really good tool for a lot of people. But a tool.

[B-ROLL]

You still get to build your life. The systems, the sleep, the boundaries, the therapy, the people around you — that's still your work. Medication just makes the work possible instead of impossible.

And here's the part that should give you your power back. You are always the one holding it. You can start. You can stop. You can adjust with your doctor. You can decide it's not for you. Nobody is doing anything *to* you.

The article wanted you to feel like medication was a force that would overwrite you. It's the opposite. It's one of the few things that finally puts *you* back in the driver's seat.

[B-ROLL]

You're not handing over control. For maybe the first time, you're taking it.
