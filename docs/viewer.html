# When Someone With ADHD Tries to "Just Relax" 😅

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When someone with ADHD tries to "just relax" and their brain opens 47 tabs instead.

You sit down. You close your eyes. You take one deep breath.

And your brain goes: "Hey, remember that embarrassing thing from 2014? Also, did you lock the door? Also, we should learn Italian. Also, are we hungry or bored? Also—"

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever been *told* to relax and immediately felt more stressed than before you started... this video is going to feel like someone read your mind.

Stick with me, because by the end you'll understand exactly why "relaxing" is so hard for your brain — and what actually works instead.

[B-ROLL]

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation. Everyone around you makes rest look effortless.

They sit down. They scroll. They nap. They "unwind." And you're over here treating a Saturday afternoon like a full-time job with no coffee breaks.

The problem isn't that you don't *want* to relax. You want it desperately. It's that the moment your body stops moving, your brain treats it as an emergency. Empty space feels dangerous. Stillness feels like something's wrong.

[B-ROLL]

So you end up "resting" while feeling guilty, restless, and somehow more exhausted than when you started.

Here's what I'm promising you today. I'm going to show you three real reasons the ADHD brain fights relaxation — and three practical swaps you can use tonight. No "just breathe." No lighting a candle and hoping.

Let's get into it.

---

BODY

Section 1: Your brain runs on stimulation, not stillness

The ADHD brain is chronically under-stimulated. It's constantly hunting for dopamine — that little hit of interest, novelty, or urgency.

So when you try to "do nothing," you're not giving your brain rest. You're putting it in withdrawal.

Think of it like a dog that needs a two-hour walk. If you make it sit in an empty room, it doesn't relax. It chews the couch. Your brain? Same energy. Sitting still with no input, it starts chewing on old memories, future worries, and random tabs.

[B-ROLL]

This is why "just sit quietly" backfires. You're not lazy for struggling with it. Your neurology is literally wired to seek input.

So the fix isn't *less* stimulation. It's the *right kind*. We'll get to that. But first, there's a second reason this is so hard.

Section 2: Rest feels unsafe when you're always behind

A lot of us live with a running list of things we *should* be doing. Dishes. Emails. That project. That text we've been ignoring for six days.

So the second you relax, guilt shows up like an uninvited roommate.

[B-ROLL]

This is what I call "productivity anxiety." Rest doesn't feel earned. It feels like slacking. So even when your body stops, your brain keeps punishing you for it.

Here's the thing though — you can't out-rest a guilty conscience. If part of you believes you don't deserve the break, the break won't work. You'll just marinate in stress on the couch.

Imagine trying to nap while someone stands over your bed saying "you have so much to do" on repeat. That's what your inner critic is doing.

[B-ROLL]

So what do we do about the guilt AND the stimulation? That leads to the third piece — the one most people get completely wrong.

Section 3: You're trying to relax the way non-ADHD people relax

Most rest advice was written for a different brain. Meditate. Sit in silence. Watch the sunset and think about nothing.

For a lot of us, "think about nothing" is genuinely impossible. Telling an ADHD brain to think about nothing is like telling a radio to play silence.

[B-ROLL]

The mistake is thinking relaxation means *emptying* your mind. For us, it usually means *occupying* it — just gently. Enough to satisfy the dopamine hunger without overwhelming the system.

That's the secret nobody tells you. ADHD rest looks different, and that's okay. It's not a weaker version of relaxing. It's just yours.

So here's what actually works.

Section 4: The swaps that actually work

Let me give you the practical part. Three swaps.

Swap one: body doubling instead of forced stillness.

Instead of sitting alone in quiet, put on a video of someone doing something calm — cooking, drawing, cleaning their room. Your brain gets low-key stimulation, and the presence of another person makes it feel safe to slow down.

[B-ROLL]

Swap two: give your hands a job so your brain can rest.

This sounds backwards, but it works. Doodling, folding laundry, a fidget, a puzzle, coloring. When your hands are busy with something low-stakes, the anxious part of your brain finally chills out.

[B-ROLL]

Rest for us often means *one* input, not *zero*. One podcast. One walk. One simple task. Not scrolling seventeen apps at once — that's overstimulation dressed up as rest.

Swap three: schedule the rest so guilt has nothing to argue with.

If guilt shows up because rest feels unearned, then make it official. Put "rest" on the list like it's a task. Set a timer. When it's on the schedule, your brain treats it as *productive*, and the guilt loses its grip.

[B-ROLL]

Sounds silly. Works shockingly well.

Section 5: The mindset shift that ties it together

Here's what I really want you to hear.

You are not broken for finding rest hard. You've just been handed a rulebook written for someone else's brain.

[B-ROLL]

Rest doesn't have to look calm to *count*. It can look like tidying while a show plays. It can look like a walk with a podcast. It can look like coloring in bed. If your nervous system settles even a little — that's a win.

Stop measuring your rest against people who don't share your wiring. Start asking, "What actually makes *me* feel recharged?"

Because the goal was never to sit perfectly still. The goal was to feel a little more human at the end of the day.
