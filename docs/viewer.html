# When ADHD People Try to Start One Simple Task 💻

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

When ADHD people try to start a task without opening 12 other tabs first.

Watch this.

You sit down to answer one email. One. Simple. Email.

[B-ROLL]

But first you need coffee. And while the coffee's brewing, you notice the sink. So now you're doing dishes. And doing dishes reminds you that you never texted your friend back. So now you're on your phone.

[B-ROLL]

Forty-five minutes later, you have 12 browser tabs open, three half-finished tasks, and that email? Still. Not. Sent.

If that felt personal, keep watching. Because I'm about to explain exactly why your brain does this — and it's not because you're lazy.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you know way too well.

You have something small to do. It should take five minutes. But between you and "done," there's a maze. And your brain builds that maze automatically.

[B-ROLL]

You didn't decide to open 12 tabs. You didn't plan to reorganize your entire desk before writing one paragraph. It just... happened. And then you blame yourself for it.

Sound familiar?

Here's the problem. Everyone keeps telling you to "just focus." "Just start." "Just do the thing."

But nobody explains why your brain treats one simple task like it's a 400-step project.

So here's what I'm going to do. In the next few minutes, I'll break down the three real reasons your ADHD brain multiplies tasks — and give you one practical trick for each. By the end, you'll actually understand the chaos. And understanding it is the first step to working with it.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: Your brain doesn't do "one step" — it does "all the steps at once"

Here's the first thing nobody tells you.

A neurotypical brain sees a task and breaks it into steps automatically. Step one, step two, step three. Your brain? It sees the entire mountain at once.

[B-ROLL]

So when you sit down to "answer one email," your brain doesn't just see the email. It sees: I need my laptop, I need to remember what they said, oh I should check the last three emails for context, wait what was that other thing I forgot, and suddenly you're overwhelmed before you've typed a single word.

Think of it like this. Imagine someone asks you to carry one grocery bag. But instead of one bag, your brain hands you all the bags at the same time. Of course you drop them. Of course you open a new tab to escape the weight.

[B-ROLL]

The trick? Shrink the task until it feels almost stupidly small. Don't write the email. Just open the email. That's it. That's the whole task.

When the mountain becomes a single pebble, your brain stops panicking and starts moving.

And speaking of that panic — let's talk about what those 12 tabs are actually doing.

Section 2: The tabs aren't distractions — they're your brain trying to hold on

This one changed everything for me.

You think the extra tabs are you getting distracted. They're not. They're your brain trying not to forget.

[B-ROLL]

ADHD working memory is like a whiteboard that erases itself every few seconds. So when a thought pops up — "oh I need to check that later" — you can't trust yourself to remember it. So you open a tab. It's an external memory. It's you trying to catch a thought before it vanishes.

The problem is, you catch every thought. And now you've got 12 open loops screaming for attention.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the fix. Get an actual place to dump those thoughts. A sticky note. A notes app. One single "brain dump" page.

When a random idea pops up mid-task, don't open a tab. Write it on the dump list. Then get back to your pebble.

You're giving your brain permission to let go — because it knows the thought is safe somewhere.

That handles the thoughts. But there's still one more reason you keep stalling. And this one's sneaky.

Section 3: Starting feels harder than the whole task actually is

This is the big one.

For ADHD brains, the hardest part of any task isn't doing it. It's starting it.

[B-ROLL]

There's a name for this. It's called activation. Your brain needs a spark of interest, urgency, or novelty to actually engage. And a boring, simple task? It offers none of those. So your brain goes, "hard pass," and quietly wanders off to do literally anything else.

That's why you'll clean the entire kitchen to avoid sending one text. It's not that the kitchen is easier. It's that the kitchen is more interesting in that moment.

[B-ROLL]

So how do you create that spark on purpose?

Try body doubling. That's when you do a boring task with another person present — in the room, on a video call, even a "study with me" video. Something about another human's presence makes your brain go, "okay fine, we're doing this now."

Or set a timer for just two minutes. Tell yourself you only have to work for two minutes. That tiny bit of urgency is often enough to trick your brain past the starting line. And once you start? You usually keep going.

[B-ROLL]

The goal isn't discipline. The goal is momentum. Get moving, and the task carries itself.

Section 4: Stop fighting your brain — build a launchpad instead

Here's what ties it all together.

You've been trying to force your brain to work like everyone else's. And every time it doesn't, you feel broken.

[B-ROLL]

But your brain isn't broken. It just needs a different runway.

So build one. Before you start a task, do three things. Shrink it to a pebble. Grab your brain-dump list. Start a two-minute timer.

[B-ROLL]

That's your launchpad. It takes ten seconds to set up, and it works with your wiring instead of against it.

Because the truth is, you don't need to become a different person. You just need a system that respects how your brain actually operates.

[B-ROLL]

One email. Sent. Finally.
