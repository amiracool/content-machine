# Rejection Sensitivity Isn't "Being Too Emotional" 💚

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

Someone says, "Can we talk later?" and your whole body goes cold.

Your brain immediately fills in the blanks. *They're mad at you. You did something wrong. This is the end.*

And here's the part nobody tells you: that's not you being dramatic. That's not you "reading too much into things."

That's a real neurological response. It even has a name. And most people — including most doctors — completely miss it.

[B-ROLL]

So if you've ever spiraled over a slightly short email, stay with me. Because by the end of this video, you're going to understand exactly what's happening in your brain — and why it was never your fault.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's what your life probably looks like.

Someone gives you feedback at work, and even though it was 90% positive, you only hear the 10%. And it haunts you for three days.

A friend takes a few hours to text back, and you've already decided they secretly can't stand you.

You avoid trying new things — not because you're lazy — but because the *idea* of failing feels physically unbearable.

[B-ROLL]

And people around you say things like, "You're too sensitive." "Stop taking everything personally." "It's not that deep."

But it *feels* that deep. Because to your nervous system, it genuinely is.

This is called Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria — or RSD. And it's one of the most common, most painful, and least talked-about parts of ADHD and neurodivergence.

By the end of this video, you'll understand three things: what RSD actually is, why your brain does this, and — most importantly — what actually helps. Not "just calm down." Real tools.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: RSD is physical pain, not just an emotion

Here's the first thing you need to understand. Rejection sensitivity isn't a mood. It's a physical response.

The word "dysphoria" literally comes from Greek — it means "unbearable." That's not an exaggeration. For a lot of neurodivergent people, the sting of rejection registers almost like actual pain.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like this. Imagine everyone else feels rejection like a paper cut. Annoying, sure, but it fades. For you, it feels like touching a hot stove. Same event. Completely different intensity.

And here's why that matters. When you touch a hot stove, you don't sit there analyzing whether you're "overreacting." You yank your hand back. That's instinct.

So when someone says, "Why are you so upset?" the honest answer is: because my brain processed that like a burn.

[B-ROLL]

That's not weakness. That's wiring. And once you understand it's a wiring thing, everything starts to make more sense.

Which brings me to the big question — *why* is your brain wired this way?

Section 2: Your brain is running an old survival program

Okay, here's where it gets kind of fascinating.

For most of human history, being rejected by your group was actually dangerous. If your tribe kicked you out, you didn't survive on your own. So the brain developed alarm systems to keep you connected — to keep you *safe*.

[B-ROLL]

For neurodivergent brains, especially ADHD brains, that alarm system tends to be turned way, way up. The volume dial is stuck on high.

So a mildly neutral text — "Can we talk later?" — trips the same ancient alarm that used to mean "you're about to be abandoned by your entire group."

Your brain isn't broken. It's actually doing its job *too* well.

[B-ROLL]

And there's a real reason for this. Many neurodivergent people grew up getting corrected constantly. Sit still. Focus. Why can't you just be normal. Stop interrupting.

By adulthood, you've heard thousands of tiny rejections. So your brain learned to scan for the next one — to see it coming, to brace for impact.

[B-ROLL]

It's a protection strategy. A painful one, but a protective one. Your brain thinks it's saving you from a threat.

The problem is, it can't always tell the difference between a real threat and a friend who's just busy.

So how do you actually work with a brain like this? That's next.

Section 3: You can't logic your way out — but you can name it

Here's the trap most people fall into.

They try to *argue* with the spiral. "There's no evidence they're mad. This is irrational. I need to stop."

And it never works. You know why? Because you're trying to reason with an alarm system. The alarm doesn't speak logic. It speaks *safety*.

[B-ROLL]

So the first tool isn't logic. It's naming.

When you feel that cold flood of rejection, try saying — out loud or in your head — "This is my RSD. This is the alarm, not the truth."

That sounds almost too simple. But naming it creates a tiny gap. A pause between the feeling and the meltdown. And in that gap, you get your choices back.

[B-ROLL]

Here's a comparison. When you know a fire drill is a *drill*, you still hear the noise. It's still loud. But you're not panicking, because you know what it is. Naming your RSD turns the real fire into a drill.

You're not pretending it doesn't hurt. You're just labeling it correctly.

And that one shift — from "I'm being rejected" to "my alarm is going off" — changes everything.

Section 4: Build a "reality gap" before you react

Now let's make it practical. Because naming it is step one. Not *acting* on it immediately is step two.

RSD wants you to react *right now*. Send the panicked text. Overapologize. Cancel the plan. Quit the thing before it can reject you.

[B-ROLL]

So build a rule for yourself: when the alarm is loudest, you don't make decisions.

I call it the reality gap. Give it time. Twenty minutes. An hour. A night's sleep if you can.

Because here's what almost always happens. The friend texts back — they were just in a meeting. The feedback turns out to be no big deal. The thing you were sure meant disaster meant nothing at all.

[B-ROLL]

And every single time your alarm is wrong, you're collecting evidence. Slowly, gently, you're teaching your brain: *maybe not every silence is a rejection.*

You won't rewire it overnight. But the gap gives your logical brain a chance to catch up to your emotional brain. And over time, that alarm gets a little quieter.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: Tell your people what's actually happening

This last one is a game-changer, and almost nobody does it.

Tell the safe people in your life about your RSD. In plain words.

Something like: "Hey — when you say 'we need to talk,' my brain panics and assumes the worst. If you could add one line about whether it's serious, that would help me so much."

[B-ROLL]

Most people have no idea their neutral messages land like a slap. They're not doing it on purpose. They just don't know your alarm is set to max.

And here's the beautiful part. The people who genuinely care about you? They'll adjust. A caring person will happily add "nothing bad, just a scheduling thing" to a text once they understand.

[B-ROLL]

That tiny change disarms the alarm before it even goes off.

You're not asking people to walk on eggshells. You're just handing them the manual to your brain. And the right people will actually be grateful you did.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So — rejection sensitivity isn't you being too emotional. It's an old alarm system, cranked too high, trying to protect you from a threat that usually isn't even there.

And now you know how to work *with* it instead of drowning in it.
