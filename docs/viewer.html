# Taking a "Quick Break" But Your Brain Has Time Blindness ⏰😅

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

"I'll just take a five-minute break."

That was four hours ago.

[B-ROLL]

The laundry is still in the washer. It's been in there since Tuesday. It's… evolving now.

[B-ROLL]

If you have ever said "quick break" and lost an entire afternoon to the void, this one's for you. Because it's not laziness. It's not bad willpower. Your brain genuinely does not experience time the way other people's brains do.

And once you understand *why*, you can finally stop hating yourself for it.

[B-ROLL]

Let's talk about it.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's your situation.

You sit down for a "quick break." You know — reward yourself. You earned it. You're a responsible adult taking a totally normal, reasonable pause.

[B-ROLL]

But somewhere between opening your phone and looking up, time didn't pass normally. It teleported. One second it's afternoon. The next, the sun is gone and you've watched a 40-minute video about how they make matches in a factory.

[B-ROLL]

This is time blindness. It's super common in ADHD brains, and a lot of neurodivergent folks deal with it. Your brain struggles to *feel* time passing. You've got two settings: "now" and "not now." That's basically it.

[B-ROLL]

So here's what I'm going to give you. By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly why your breaks explode into hours — and three real strategies to take a break *without* losing your whole day.

Not willpower tips. Not "just try harder." Actual tools that work *with* your brain.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: Your Brain Only Knows "Now" and "Not Now"

Most people have an internal clock that quietly ticks in the background. They can *feel* twenty minutes pass. Your brain? It doesn't have that running in the background.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like driving a car with no fuel gauge. You're not being reckless. You just have no idea how much gas is left until you're stranded on the side of the road. That's a "quick break" with time blindness. You feel completely fine… right up until four hours have vanished.

[B-ROLL]

This is why "I'll only be a minute" is one of the funniest lies your brain tells you. You're not lying on purpose. You genuinely believed it. Your brain just has no way to measure "a minute."

[B-ROLL]

And the worst part? When you finally snap out of it, you feel like garbage. Guilt, shame, the whole combo meal.

So the first fix isn't a hack. It's this: stop treating time blindness like a character flaw. It's a wiring thing. Once you believe that, the actual strategies start to work. Which brings us to the next one.

Section 2: Make Time *Visible* Instead of Invisible

If your brain can't *feel* time, you have to let it *see* time.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the trick. A regular digital clock does nothing for you. You look at "5:42" and your brain goes, "Cool, that means nothing to me." Numbers are abstract. Your brain needs something physical. Something it can watch disappear.

[B-ROLL]

This is where a visual timer changes your life. One of those ones where you can literally watch a colored section shrink. As the red part gets smaller, your brain goes, "Oh. *Oh.* Time is a real thing that is actively running out." It becomes a feeling instead of a number.

[B-ROLL]

It's like the difference between someone telling you "you have twenty dollars left" versus watching actual cash get pulled out of your hand one bill at a time. One is information. The other is *panic you can feel.* And we want that little bit of friendly panic.

[B-ROLL]

So before your break, set the visual timer. Put it where you can see it. Not on your phone — your phone is the void. A separate timer across the room. When that color's gone, break's over.

But even that only works if the alarm actually *interrupts* you. Which is the next problem.

Section 3: Build a "Hard Stop," Not a Soft One

A gentle little *ding* will not save you.

[B-ROLL]

Here's why. When you're in a dopamine loop — scrolling, gaming, watching, whatever — a soft alarm is basically background noise. You swat it away without even thinking. "I'll stop after this one." Famous last words.

[B-ROLL]

You need a hard stop. Something that makes continuing *more annoying* than stopping. This is the key. Don't rely on motivation. Rely on friction.

[B-ROLL]

Real examples. Set an alarm in another room so you physically have to get up to turn it off. Once you're up, you're up — the spell is broken. Or use a smart speaker to announce out loud, "Break is over, start the dishes." Hearing an actual instruction hits different than a buzz.

[B-ROLL]

Or my favorite — stack your break *before* something you can't skip. Take your break right before you have to leave for work, or before a call. Now time blindness has a hard wall it literally cannot pass through.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a playground with a fence. You're still free to run around and have fun. The fence just stops you from wandering off a cliff. The hard stop is your fence.

[B-ROLL]

And here's the bonus — when you know the fence exists, you actually relax *more* during your break. Because part of your brain isn't quietly stressing about losing control. The structure gives you permission to enjoy it.

Which leads to the last, most important shift.

Section 4: Give the Break a Finish Line

Open-ended breaks are a trap.

[B-ROLL]

"I'll just relax for a bit" has no finish line. And a brain without a finish line will run forever, because there's nothing telling it to stop. Time blindness loves a vague plan. It feasts on it.

[B-ROLL]

So define the break by a *thing*, not by *time*. Instead of "I'll scroll for a while," say "I'll watch exactly two videos." Instead of "I'll chill," say "I'll drink one cup of tea, and when it's empty, I'm up."

[B-ROLL]

Object-based limits work way better than time-based ones for our brains, because they're concrete. You can *see* the tea run out. You can *count* the two videos. It gives your "now/not now" brain a clear edge to grab onto.

[B-ROLL]

It's like the difference between "swim for a while" and "swim to that buoy and come back." One of those you can actually finish. The other you'll drift forever in.

[B-ROLL]

Stack all four of these together — accept the wiring, make time visible, build a hard stop, and set a finish line — and the four-hour black hole break becomes a real, restful, *20-minute* break. That you actually chose.

[B-ROLL]

And the laundry? Finally gets done. While it's still technically laundry.

[B-ROLL]
