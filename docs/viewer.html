# My Response to the Therapist Who Said Autism and ADHD Can't Coexist

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

A therapist once looked me dead in the eyes and said, "You can't be both. Pick one."

Autism *or* ADHD. Not both.

I sat there in that beige little office, holding two assessment reports — from two different specialists — that both said otherwise. And this professional, someone I paid to help me understand my own brain, told me the reports were basically... wrong.

[B-ROLL]

Here's the thing. That therapist wasn't just wrong. They were wrong in a way that keeps millions of people undiagnosed, misdiagnosed, and quietly convinced there's something broken in them that no one can name.

So today, I'm responding. And I'm bringing the receipts.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

If you've ever felt like you're *two contradictions in one body* — this is for you.

You crave routine, but you also can't stick to one.

You hyperfocus for six hours straight, then can't start a task for six days.

You want deep, structured order... and you have twelve unfinished projects on your desk right now.

[B-ROLL]

For years, you might've been told you're "too functional" to be autistic. Or "too rigid" to have ADHD. And so you fell into the gap between the two — a gap where a huge number of people live without ever getting answers.

In the next few minutes, I'm going to show you exactly why autism and ADHD *do* coexist, what the actual research says, and how understanding this combination can completely change how you treat yourself.

By the end, you'll have language for something you've probably felt your whole life but never had a name for.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: The label that changes everything — "AuDHD"

Here's the first thing that therapist didn't know, or didn't care to know: there's a name for the overlap. People call it AuDHD.

It's not a niche internet fantasy. It's the lived reality of being autistic *and* having ADHD at the same time.

Think of it like this. Imagine driving a car with one foot pressing the accelerator and the other slamming the brake. The ADHD side wants novelty, speed, stimulation — *go, go, go.* The autistic side wants predictability, sameness, control — *stop, plan, protect.*

[B-ROLL]

That internal tug-of-war isn't you being lazy or dramatic. It's two operating systems running on the same machine.

And once you see it that way, the "contradictions" stop being contradictions. They start being an explanation.

Which brings me to the actual science — because this isn't just a feeling.

Section 2: The research the "pick one" crowd ignored

For decades, the official diagnostic manuals literally *forbade* diagnosing both. Until 2013, you couldn't be given an autism and an ADHD diagnosis together. Not because they didn't coexist — because a book said you weren't allowed.

[B-ROLL]

So a whole generation of clinicians was trained on that rule. Including, probably, the one who told me to "pick one."

But the research caught up. Studies now consistently estimate that somewhere between 30 and 80% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD, depending on the group studied. That is not a rounding error. That is a massive, overlapping population.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like being left-handed and having blue eyes. Two separate traits. No rule of the universe says you can only have one. They just... co-occur. Often.

So when someone says "you can't be both," what they're really telling you is that their education stopped in 2013.

And that leads to the part that actually hurts the most.

Section 3: Why getting told "pick one" does real damage

When you're forced to choose one label, you get treatment designed for *half* of your brain.

[B-ROLL]

Say they treat you as "just ADHD." They put you on stimulant medication and tell you to build more structure. But nobody accounts for your sensory overwhelm, your need for downtime, your burnout from masking all day. So the meds help you focus... on a life that's still quietly draining you.

Or flip it. They treat you as "just autistic." They focus on routines and social scripts, but ignore that your brain is genuinely under-stimulated and desperate for novelty. So you follow the perfect plan and still feel like you're crawling out of your skin.

[B-ROLL]

It's like getting glasses that fix one eye and leaving the other blurry. Technically an improvement. Still not the full picture.

The cost isn't just inconvenience. It's years. Years of thinking, *why doesn't the standard advice work for me?* Years of blaming yourself when the real problem was a label that was too small.

And here's what almost nobody tells you about that.

Section 4: The self-blame that comes from living in the gap

When your brain doesn't fit neatly into one box, you start to believe the problem is *you.*

Not the box. You.

[B-ROLL]

You think, "Other people with ADHD seem to thrive on spontaneity — why do I panic when plans change?" And, "Other autistic people seem to love their routines — why can't I stick to mine for more than three days?"

So you decide you must be doing *both* wrong. You're a "bad" version of each.

Here's the reframe that changed everything for me. You're not a bad version of either. You're a *whole* version of both. The panic when plans change *and* the boredom with routine — those aren't failures. They're exactly what you'd expect from a brain wired for both order and novelty at the same time.

[B-ROLL]

Once I understood that, I stopped fighting myself. I started building a life with *both* systems in mind. Structure that has flexibility built in. Novelty that lives inside safe routines.

That's the part I wish that therapist had helped me find. So let me hand it to you directly.

Section 5: What actually helps when you're both

You don't have to choose which half of your brain to serve. You design for the whole thing.

[B-ROLL]

Build routines — but leave room for choice inside them. Instead of "gym at 6pm every day," try "movement daily — you pick which kind." You get the predictability autism wants and the novelty ADHD needs.

Protect your energy *and* your stimulation. Give yourself sensory recovery time after busy days, and give yourself something new and exciting to look forward to. Both. Not one or the other.

[B-ROLL]

And most importantly — find people, and professionals, who understand the overlap exists. If a clinician tells you to "pick one," that's not a diagnosis. That's a red flag. You are allowed to seek someone who's actually kept up with the last decade of research.

Because the goal was never to fit into a box. The goal is to finally understand the machine you've been driving your whole life.

CTA (final 30 seconds)

So that's my response. To the therapist, and to anyone who's ever told you your brain has to be simpler than it is.

You're not too much. You're not doing it wrong. You're just running two systems at once — and once you get that, everything makes more sense.

[B-ROLL]

If this hit home, subscribe — because this channel exists to give you the language nobody handed you sooner. That's the whole point.

And next, go watch my video on autistic burnout versus ADHD shutdown — because if you're AuDHD, you've almost certainly experienced both, and confused one for the other. It'll click.

I'll see you there.
