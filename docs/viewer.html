# My ADHD Brain Forgets Your Birthday But Recites a 2009 Jingle Perfectly 🤣

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

My ADHD brain may not remember your birthday but… it will recite a jingle from a 2009 cereal commercial. Word for word. In perfect pitch.

[B-ROLL]

You know what I mean. Somebody's birthday? Gone. Blank. The date evaporated the second they told me.

But that random song from a car insurance ad thirteen years ago? Locked in the vault. Permanent resident. Paying no rent.

[B-ROLL]

If your brain also stores the wrong things with maximum effort… stay right here. Because this isn't a you problem. This is a brain thing. And it actually makes sense once you understand it.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you're living in.

You forget appointments. You forget names two seconds after hearing them. You walk into a room and forget why. You've apologized a thousand times for "having a bad memory."

[B-ROLL]

But then… you'll quote an entire movie you saw once. You'll remember a stranger's obscure comment from a party three years ago. You'll hum a commercial that hasn't aired since flip phones were cool.

And people say, "How do you remember THAT but you forgot my name?"

[B-ROLL]

So you start thinking you're careless. Or lazy. Or that you just don't care about people.

Here's the promise. By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly why your brain does this. You'll stop feeling broken about it. And you'll get three real strategies to remember the things that actually matter — without fighting your brain the whole way.

Let's go.

BODY

Section 1: Your brain doesn't store memories by importance — it stores them by emotion

Here's the core thing nobody told you. The ADHD brain doesn't file memories based on how important something is. It files them based on how it *felt* in that moment.

[B-ROLL]

A birthday date is just… a number. Neutral. Boring to the brain. There's no dopamine spike attached to "March 14th."

But that 2009 jingle? It had a catchy melody. It surprised you. It made you feel something — even if that something was just "this is stupidly catchy." Your brain went, *ooh, dopamine,* and stapled it to your soul forever.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a bouncer at a club. Your brain's bouncer doesn't let in "the responsible stuff." It lets in whatever's loud, weird, or emotional. Facts have to fight for their life at the door.

So no — you're not careless. Your memory filter is just tuned to a completely different setting than most people expect.

That leads to the next part, which is why the boring stuff never even gets a shot.

Section 2: Boring information doesn't get "encoded" — it never really lands

There's a step before memory called encoding. It's when your brain actually decides to save something. And for ADHD brains, low-stimulation information often skips the save button entirely.

[B-ROLL]

When your coworker says "the meeting moved to Thursday," your brain hears it… but it never encodes it. It's like typing a whole message and then never hitting send. The words happened. The memory didn't form.

[B-ROLL]

That's why you can be looking directly at someone, genuinely listening, and still have zero memory of what they said. You weren't ignoring them. Your brain just didn't press record.

Compare that to the jingle. That thing had rhythm, repetition, novelty, and emotion all at once. It kicked the encoding door open. It didn't ask permission.

[B-ROLL]

So the trick isn't trying harder to remember. Trying harder doesn't work — you've been trying harder your whole life. The trick is making the boring stuff *interesting enough to get encoded in the first place.*

Which brings us to what you can actually do about it.

Section 3: Make the boring thing weird, and your brain will finally keep it

If your brain only saves emotional or novel information, then your job is simple. Stop feeding it plain facts. Feed it weird ones.

[B-ROLL]

Someone's birthday is March 14th? Boring. Won't stick. But "March 14th is Pi Day, so I'll picture them holding a giant pie"? Now it's weird. Now there's an image. Now it has a chance.

[B-ROLL]

This is why mnemonics work so well for us. We're not bad at memory — we're bad at *boring* memory. The instant you make something visual, silly, or exaggerated, you've bribed the bouncer.

[B-ROLL]

Try it with names too. Meet a guy named Mike? Picture him holding a giant microphone, singing badly. Ridiculous? Yes. That's the point. Ridiculous is memorable. Normal is forgettable.

Your brain rewards the strange. So give it strange on purpose.

Section 4: Stop storing things in your brain — outsource them completely

Here's the reframe that changes everything. Your ADHD brain is amazing at *ideas* and terrible at *storage.* So stop using it as storage.

[B-ROLL]

You would never expect a race car to also be a moving truck. Your brain is the race car. Fast, creative, exciting — and absolutely not built to haul cargo like dates, times, and to-do lists.

So the cargo goes external. Every single time.

[B-ROLL]

Birthdays go into your calendar the *second* you hear them — with an alert two days early so you have time to actually do something. Not the day of. Two days early. That buffer is the whole game.

[B-ROLL]

Tasks go into your phone or on a sticky note the moment they appear — not "later," because later doesn't exist for us. The thought lives for about four seconds. Catch it before it dies.

[B-ROLL]

And here's the mindset shift. Writing it down isn't cheating. It isn't a sign you're failing. It's you designing a system that fits your actual brain instead of the one you were told to have.

[B-ROLL]

Section 5: Give yourself grace — you're not broken, you're just wired differently

This is the part I really want you to hear. All those years you thought you were careless, or that you didn't care enough about people? That was never true.

[B-ROLL]

You cared. You just had a memory system that runs on a different fuel. And nobody handed you the manual.

The friend who forgets your birthday but remembers the exact thing that made you laugh five years ago? That friend loves you deeply. The memory just filed itself under "made me feel something" instead of "important date."

[B-ROLL]

So the next time your brain proudly recites a jingle from 2009 while blanking on something "important," don't get frustrated. Laugh at it. That's just your bouncer doing its weird little job.

[B-ROLL]

And then quietly open your calendar and add the important thing. Weird image and all.

That's not fighting your brain. That's finally working *with* it.
