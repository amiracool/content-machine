# Every autistic adult does THIS to survive small talk 🗣️

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

Every autistic adult has a secret script running in their head during small talk.

You know the one.

"Say hi. Ask how they are. Nod. Don't stare too long. Wait — did I stare too long? Okay, laugh now. Not too loud. Mirror their energy. Are my hands weird? Why are my hands weird?"

[B-ROLL]

If your brain does that during a two-minute conversation about the weather, you're not broken. You're masking. And today I'm going to show you exactly what you're doing, why it's exhausting you, and how to make it stop draining your battery.

Stick around, because point number three is the one nobody talks about.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation.

You can hold a deep, hours-long conversation about a topic you love. Effortlessly. But then a coworker says "How was your weekend?" and suddenly you feel like you're defusing a bomb.

[B-ROLL]

That's not a contradiction. That's the reality of being autistic in a world built for neurotypical conversation.

The problem is this: small talk isn't about information. It's about rhythm, timing, and unspoken rules. And nobody handed you the rulebook. So you built your own. Piece by piece. Over years. Usually in silence.

That homemade rulebook is called masking. And while it helps you fit in, it comes with a bill. Exhaustion. Anxiety. That hollow feeling after a "successful" chat where you have no idea if you were actually yourself.

[B-ROLL]

By the end of this video, you'll understand the four things you're secretly doing to survive small talk — and one shift that lets you keep the connection while spending way less energy.

Let's break it down.

---

BODY

Section 1: You pre-write the entire conversation

The first thing almost every autistic adult does is rehearse.

Before a phone call, a party, or even a quick chat at the desk, you run simulations. You imagine what they'll say. You prepare answers. You have backup answers for the backup answers.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a chess player planning ten moves ahead. Except the game is "How was your weekend," and the stakes feel like your entire social reputation.

Here's the catch. The moment the conversation goes off-script — they mention something you didn't prepare for — the whole system stalls. That awkward pause? That's your brain frantically loading a new page.

[B-ROLL]

It works. But it means you're never really *in* the moment. You're always three seconds ahead of it. And that's exhausting.

Which leads to the second thing.

Section 2: You copy other people's behavior in real time

This is called mirroring. And you're probably a master at it without even realizing.

You watch how the other person stands, how fast they talk, how much they smile — and you match it. Their energy goes up, yours goes up. They get quiet, you get quiet.

[B-ROLL]

It's like being a human chameleon. You blend in by becoming a reflection of whoever's in front of you.

The problem? A chameleon doesn't have a color of its own. If you spend every conversation reflecting other people, you can end up with no idea who *you* actually are in a room.

[B-ROLL]

I've had autistic adults tell me they came home from a party and genuinely couldn't remember which version of themselves they'd been all night. That's the cost of nonstop mirroring.

And it feeds directly into number three — the one nobody warns you about.

Section 3: You monitor yourself instead of the conversation

Here's the hidden truth.

While a neurotypical person is mostly focused on the *other* person, you're often focused on *yourself*. Constantly. Am I making enough eye contact? Too much? Is my face doing the right thing? Did that joke land? Was that too honest?

[B-ROLL]

It's like trying to drive a car while also watching yourself drive on a security monitor. You're doing two full-time jobs at once. No wonder ten minutes of small talk feels like running a marathon.

This is the part people miss. When someone says autistic folks are "bad at social stuff," they've got it backwards. You're not doing *less* social processing. You're doing *way more*. You're running two brains where most people run one.

[B-ROLL]

And that self-monitoring is the number one reason small talk drains you. It's not the talking. It's the surveillance.

Now here's the fourth thing — and the one that finally starts to explain the crash.

Section 4: You delay the meltdown until you're alone

You hold it together. Perfectly. Smile, nod, respond, exit gracefully.

And then you get to your car. Or your bathroom. Or your bed. And *everything* hits at once.

[B-ROLL]

This is delayed processing. All the energy you spent suppressing your natural reactions? It doesn't disappear. It waits. It piles up. And it collects the bill the second you're safe.

Think of it like holding your breath underwater. You *can* hold it. You look calm on the surface. But the moment you break through, you gasp. That gasp is the shutdown. The exhaustion. Sometimes the tears that come out of nowhere.

[B-ROLL]

If you've ever wondered why you're wiped out after a normal social day when nothing "bad" happened — this is why. Nothing bad happened *out loud*. It all happened inside.

So what do you actually do about it? Here's the shift.

Section 5: The one change that saves your battery

You don't have to stop masking overnight. That's not realistic, and honestly, some masking keeps you safe. The goal isn't zero mask. The goal is *choosing* when you mask instead of running it on autopilot 24/7.

[B-ROLL]

Start with one small thing: pick one safe person or one safe situation, and let yourself drop *one* rule. Maybe you stop forcing eye contact with a close friend. Maybe you say "I'm not great at small talk, can we just talk about something real?" — and watch how many people say "oh thank god, me too."

[B-ROLL]

Because here's the thing nobody tells you. A lot of people are relieved when you skip the script. Weather talk exhausts *everyone*. When you're honest, you give them permission to be honest too.

You're not asking the whole world to accommodate you. You're just building small pockets where you don't have to run two brains at once. And those pockets are where you finally get to rest.

[B-ROLL]

That one change — going from automatic masking to intentional masking — is what turns social interaction from a battery drain into something you can actually recover from.
