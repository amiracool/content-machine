# When ADHD Brains Try to Start a Task They Actually WANT to Do 🤦

---

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

You've been excited about this ALL week.

You cleared your schedule. You told your friends. This is the thing you actually *want* to do.

So why are you standing in the middle of your room… doing absolutely nothing?

[B-ROLL]

Here's the part nobody tells you. The tasks that are hardest for ADHD brains aren't the boring ones.

They're the ones you care about the most.

[B-ROLL]

Let me explain why — because once you understand this, you're going to stop hating yourself for it.

---

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Okay, be honest.

You've got a hobby you love. A passion project. A book you swore you'd write. A guitar collecting dust. A game you were dying to play.

And you keep… not doing it.

[B-ROLL]

Then the guilt shows up. "What is wrong with me? I *wanted* this. I'm just lazy."

Stop right there.

[B-ROLL]

You're not lazy. Your brain runs on a different fuel system than most people realize. And when the stakes feel high — even in a good way — that system stalls.

Here's what I'm going to do for you today.

I'm going to break down the three real reasons your ADHD brain freezes on the stuff you love. And I'm going to give you one small trick for each that actually works.

By the end, you'll have a way to start — even when your brain is screaming "not yet."

Let's get into it.

---

BODY

Section 1: The task is too big and your brain can't see step one

Your brain doesn't see a task. It sees a giant, glowing blob of "everything at once."

[B-ROLL]

Neurotypical brains tend to break things into steps automatically. ADHD brains? We often skip straight to the finished product. You imagine the whole novel. The perfect painting. The finished song.

And when you can't see step one, you freeze. Because your brain can't act on a blob.

Think of it like being told to "eat an elephant." Nobody eats an elephant. You eat one bite. But your brain keeps trying to fit the whole elephant in its mouth.

[B-ROLL]

The trick: Make step one so small it feels stupid.

Not "write the chapter." Just "open the document." Not "practice guitar." Just "take the guitar out of the case." Not "start the workout." Just "put on the shoes."

[B-ROLL]

The goal isn't to finish. The goal is to *touch* the thing. Momentum comes after, not before.

And here's the sneaky part — once step one is done, step two usually appears on its own.

So the blob is problem number one. But there's a second reason that's even more painful.

Section 2: You care too much, so your brain treats it like a threat

This one hits deep.

The task you love carries weight. It *means* something to you. And that meaning is exactly what makes it scary.

[B-ROLL]

Because if you try and it's bad? That's not just a bad painting. That feels like proof you're not who you hoped you were.

So your brain does the "smart" thing. It protects you. It keeps you from starting, because if you never start, you can never fail.

[B-ROLL]

This is called the perfectionism trap, and ADHD brains fall into it hard. The dream stays perfect in your head. Reality feels risky. So you scroll instead.

[B-ROLL]

The trick: Give yourself permission to be bad on purpose.

Say it out loud. "I'm going to make the worst first draft ever." "I'm going to play this song terribly." "This first sketch is going in the trash and that's the plan."

[B-ROLL]

When you remove the pressure to be good, you remove the threat. And when the threat is gone, your brain lets you begin.

You're not making a masterpiece. You're making a mess. And a mess you can always fix. A blank page you can't.

Now… let's talk about the third reason. This is the one that surprises people the most.

Section 3: There's no urgency, so your brain won't release the fuel

Here's the truth about ADHD motivation.

Your brain doesn't run on importance. It runs on interest, urgency, novelty, and challenge.

[B-ROLL]

That's why you can somehow write a whole essay the night before it's due. But the project with no deadline — the one you *actually* love — sits there for months.

Because there's no urgency. Nobody's waiting. There's no pressure firing off the chemicals you need to start.

[B-ROLL]

The stuff you love is often "someday" stuff. And "someday" is not a fuel your brain can burn.

The trick: Create fake urgency and fake accountability.

Set a timer for 10 minutes and race it. Text a friend "I'm starting now, check on me at 3." Book a slot in your calendar like it's a real appointment you can't cancel.

[B-ROLL]

Or use body doubling — do the task while someone else is present, even on a video call, just quietly existing next to you. The presence of another person creates enough gentle pressure to get moving.

[B-ROLL]

You're not lazy. You're just missing the spark. So you build the spark yourself.

Section 4: Put it together — the 3-step "just start" formula

So next time you're frozen in front of the thing you love, run this:

[B-ROLL]

One. Shrink it. Find the stupidly small first step. Open the doc. Grab the shoes.

Two. Suck on purpose. Promise yourself the worst version possible. Kill the perfection.

Three. Add pressure. Timer, friend, calendar, or body double. Manufacture the urgency your brain needs.

[B-ROLL]

That's it. Not "try harder." Not "want it more." You already want it. That was never the problem.

The problem was your brain needed a different on-ramp. And now you've got one.
