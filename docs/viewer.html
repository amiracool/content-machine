# The Weird Thing Every Autistic Adult Does in Conversations 🧠

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

There's a thing you do in almost every conversation.

You've probably never told anyone about it. Because you assumed everyone did it too.

But they don't.

You rehearse conversations before they happen. And then you replay them for hours after they're over.

If your brain just went "wait… that's not normal?" — stay right here.

Because what I'm about to tell you is going to explain about twenty years of your life.

[B-ROLL]

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the situation you're living in.

You walk into a coffee shop. Before you even open the door, you've already run the whole interaction in your head. What you'll say. What they'll say back. What you'll say if they say something unexpected.

And then you order… and it goes slightly off-script… and your brain short-circuits.

[B-ROLL]

Then later that night — you're in bed — and suddenly you're replaying a conversation from six years ago. Cringing. Wondering if that one thing you said made everyone secretly hate you.

If that's you, this isn't a personality flaw. And it's definitely not "being awkward."

It's called scripting. And it's one of the most common — and most hidden — traits of autistic adults.

By the end of this video, you're going to understand exactly why your brain does this, why it's actually a superpower in disguise, and how to stop it from exhausting you.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: Scripting isn't overthinking — it's translating

Here's the first thing nobody tells you.

When you rehearse a conversation, you're not being neurotic. You're doing real-time translation.

[B-ROLL]

For a lot of neurotypical people, conversation is like breathing. It just happens. The words come out and match the intention.

For a lot of autistic people, it's more like speaking a second language you learned from a textbook. You know the grammar. You know the vocabulary. But you have to actively assemble the sentence before it's safe to say out loud.

Think about it like a stage actor. An actor doesn't wing Shakespeare. They rehearse until it feels natural. You do the same thing — except your "play" is asking someone where the bathroom is.

[B-ROLL]

So the scripting isn't a bug. It's the tool your brain built to keep up in a world that runs on unspoken rules.

But here's the problem with scripts. They only work until someone goes off-book.

Which brings me to the thing that actually hurts.

Section 2: The "off-script panic" is real — and it has a name

You planned the conversation. You were ready.

And then the other person said something you didn't predict.

[B-ROLL]

And your whole system just… froze.

That blank, panicky, "I have no idea what to do with my face" moment? That's not stupidity. That's your brain hitting a page in the script that doesn't exist.

Here's an analogy. Imagine you're a GPS. You've mapped the entire route. Then someone throws up a road closure with no detour. A normal GPS recalculates in half a second. Yours takes… a minute. In front of everyone. While they're waiting.

[B-ROLL]

And in that minute, you feel every single second.

This is why unexpected phone calls feel like an ambush. It's why small talk is so much harder than deep conversation — small talk is unpredictable, but deep topics you've already thought about a thousand times.

You're not bad at talking. You're bad at improvising under surveillance. Those are completely different skills.

And when the conversation is finally over? That's when the second part kicks in.

Section 3: The post-conversation replay loop

This is the part that steals your sleep.

The conversation ends. Everyone else moves on with their day. And you? You start the replay.

[B-ROLL]

You go back over every word. Did that joke land? Did I talk too much? Why did I say "you too" when the waiter said "enjoy your meal"?

You will analyze one three-second interaction for three hours.

Here's what's actually happening. Your brain is doing quality control. It's trying to update the script for next time. It's saying, "Let's review the footage so we don't make that mistake again."

The problem is your brain doesn't know when to stop reviewing. It just keeps hitting rewind.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a coach reviewing game tape. Useful in small doses. But imagine watching the same ten-second clip on loop until three in the morning. That's not analysis anymore. That's torture.

And the cruelest part? The neurotypical person you talked to forgot the whole thing within minutes. The mistake you're still cringing about? They literally do not remember it exists.

So why does your brain do all of this in the first place? Let's go deeper.

Section 4: It all comes back to safety

Here's the thing underneath everything I've said.

Scripting, panic, replaying — they're all the same thing. They're safety behaviors.

[B-ROLL]

Somewhere along the way, you learned that getting a conversation "wrong" had consequences. Maybe you got laughed at. Maybe you got the look — that little flicker of confusion on someone's face when you said something "weird." Maybe you got excluded and never understood why.

So your brain did what brains do. It built armor.

The rehearsing is armor before the fight. The replaying is armor being repaired after the fight. Your nervous system decided that conversation is dangerous, so it never stops preparing for battle.

[B-ROLL]

And here's the part I really need you to hear. This is not you being broken. This is you being adaptive. You built an entire system to survive a world that wasn't designed for how your brain works. That's not weakness. That's engineering.

But you can't live in armor forever. It's too heavy. So let's talk about what actually helps.

Section 5: You don't stop scripting — you make peace with it

Here's what I'm not going to tell you. I'm not going to tell you to "just be spontaneous" or "stop overthinking." That advice is useless and honestly a little insulting.

[B-ROLL]

Instead, three things that actually work.

One. Give yourself permission to script. Stop fighting it. If preparing for a phone call makes it possible, then prepare for the phone call. A wheelchair isn't a failure to walk. Your scripts aren't a failure to socialize. They're a tool. Use them without shame.

[B-ROLL]

Two. Build one "escape phrase" for when the script breaks. Something you can say on autopilot when your brain freezes. Like, "Ooh, let me think about that for a sec." That single phrase buys you the recalculation time your brain needs — out loud, without panic. Most people read it as thoughtful, not weird.

[B-ROLL]

Three. Set a replay curfew. When the nighttime loop starts, tell yourself: "I'll review this tomorrow at 5pm for exactly ten minutes." Sounds silly. But it works, because your brain isn't scared of the memory — it's scared of forgetting to learn from it. Give it a scheduled time, and it can finally let go.

[B-ROLL]

You are not going to become a different person. And you shouldn't have to. The goal was never to talk like everyone else. The goal is to stop punishing yourself for talking like you.
