# Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria: The Hidden Reason Small Criticisms Feel Physically Unbearable

HOOK (0–30 seconds)

Someone sends you a text that just says "we need to talk."

And suddenly your chest is tight. Your stomach drops. Your brain is already writing the worst-case ending.

It wasn't even bad news. But your body reacted like you'd been slapped.

If a tiny bit of criticism can wreck your entire day... if one cold look from a friend can make you want to disappear... you're not "too sensitive."

You might be dealing with something that has an actual name. And almost nobody talks about it.

[B-ROLL]

Let me explain what's really happening inside you.

SETUP (30–90 seconds)

Here's the thing nobody told you.

For a lot of people with ADHD, rejection doesn't feel like an emotion. It feels like pain. Real, physical pain.

It's called Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria. RSD. And once you learn about it, your whole life starts to make sense.

The time you rehearsed a text message for twenty minutes. The way you replay an awkward conversation at 2am. The reason you'd rather say nothing than risk someone being disappointed in you.

That's not a character flaw. That's a nervous system wired to treat criticism like a threat.

[B-ROLL]

In this video, I'm going to explain exactly what RSD is, why it hits so hard, and three things you can actually do about it. By the end, you'll finally have words for something you've felt your whole life.

Let's get into it.

BODY

Section 1: RSD Isn't "Being Dramatic" — It's A Pain Response

Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria is an intense, almost unbearable emotional reaction to the feeling of being rejected, criticized, or falling short.

And I mean intense. People describe it like a physical wound. A punch to the gut. A wave of heat in the face.

Here's an analogy. Imagine everyone else has a dimmer switch on their emotions. A small criticism turns the dial up a little. Annoying, but manageable.

With RSD, there's no dimmer. There's just an on-off switch. And a little comment flips it straight to maximum. Zero to a hundred, instantly.

[B-ROLL]

So when you feel like you "overreacted"? You didn't choose that. Your brain delivered a full-volume response to a low-volume event.

And that's the part most people get wrong. They think it's about being fragile. It's not. It's about intensity you can't control.

Now, where does this come from?

Section 2: Years Of Small Rejections Build A Hidden Scar

Here's what makes RSD worse over time.

If you grew up with undiagnosed ADHD, you heard a lot of correction. "Why can't you focus?" "You're not trying hard enough." "Why are you so forgetful?"

One comment is nothing. But thousands of them? That teaches your brain one thing: I'm always one mistake away from being rejected.

[B-ROLL]

Think of it like a smoke detector that's been triggered too many times. Eventually it gets so sensitive it goes off when you make toast.

Your rejection alarm works the same way. After enough real rejections, it starts firing at imaginary ones. A friend takes an hour to text back, and the alarm screams "they hate you now."

[B-ROLL]

You're not paranoid. Your system got trained by experience. And that's actually good news, because anything that's learned can be worked with.

So let's talk about how it secretly runs your life.

Section 3: The Two Masks RSD Makes You Wear

RSD doesn't just hurt. It changes your behavior in ways you might not even notice.

Most people cope in one of two ways.

The first is the people-pleaser. You avoid rejection by becoming whatever everyone needs. You say yes when you mean no. You apologize for things that aren't your fault. You read every room for the first sign someone's upset with you.

[B-ROLL]

The second is the avoider. If you never try, you can never fail. So you don't apply for the job. You don't send the message. You don't show people your real work, because if they reject it, they're rejecting you.

Here's the painful part. Both masks look like success from the outside. The people-pleaser seems kind and easygoing. The avoider seems chill and low-maintenance.

Inside, both are exhausted from managing a threat nobody else can see.

[B-ROLL]

If you saw yourself in either of those... keep watching. Because now I'm going to give you something that actually helps.

Section 4: How To Catch RSD In The Moment

You can't stop the first wave. That's chemistry. But you can change what happens next.

Step one: name it. The second that pain hits, say to yourself, "This is RSD. This is the alarm, not the truth."

That sounds small. It's not. Naming it creates a tiny gap between the feeling and your reaction. And that gap is where all your power lives.

[B-ROLL]

Step two: check the evidence. RSD lies with confidence. It tells you your friend hates you. So ask: what's the actual proof? Usually it's one neutral text and a mountain of your imagination.

Here's an analogy. RSD is like a lawyer who only argues one side. Your job is to be the judge who asks, "Okay, but where's the real evidence?"

[B-ROLL]

Step three: delay your response. Feeling rejected makes you want to react right now. Apologize right now. Quit right now. Don't. Give it an hour. The intensity always fades, and you make much better choices on the other side of that wave.

Name it. Check it. Delay it. Three steps. You won't nail them every time, and that's fine. Progress, not perfection.

Section 5: Why Understanding This Changes Everything

Here's what I really want you to hear.

For years you probably thought something was wrong with you. That you were too much. Too sensitive. Too emotional. You might have hidden this part of yourself from everyone you love.

But RSD isn't a flaw in your character. It's a feature of how your brain processes emotion. And the same wiring that makes rejection hurt so much? It's often the same wiring that makes you deeply empathetic, incredibly loyal, and tuned in to other people in a way most folks never are.

[B-ROLL]

You feel rejection intensely because you feel everything intensely. That's not broken. That's the cost of a big heart running on a sensitive system.

[B-ROLL]

So the goal isn't to stop feeling. It's to stop letting a false alarm run your decisions. And now you have the words, the reason, and the tools to start.
